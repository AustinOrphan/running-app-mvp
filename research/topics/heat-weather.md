# Heat, humidity and weather

## Heat, humidity and weather: what the engine can use

All 13 researcher entries verified (DOIs resolve; Crossref titles, journals and first authors match; abstracts fetched from PubMed or Europe PMC). None was dropped. Three papers were added: [periard-2015-heat-acclimation-adaptations], [knechtle-2019-boston-weather] and [struska-2026-ultra-weather-sex]. Two researcher numbers were corrected: the Ely 2007 penalties relative to the coolest band are +0.8, +1.6 and +2.8% (not +1.0%), and Parise's slowest group is 24-30 h.

### Well established

- **Heat slows endurance running, and slower runners lose more finishing time.** Race-level data agree across marathons [ely-2007-weather-marathon][elhelou-2012-environment-marathon][vihma-2010-weather-marathon][knechtle-2019-boston-weather], with slowing already visible between WBGT 5 and 25 C. Evidence is observational but replicated, so grade C.
- **WBGT (or an input that includes solar load) beats air temperature alone** for explaining elite results [mantzios-2022-weather-endurance], and solar radiation correlates with slowing [vihma-2010-weather-marathon].
- **Heat acclimation is the main countermeasure.** Consensus [racinais-2015-heat-consensus] and meta-analysis [tyler-2016-heat-adaptation-meta] agree on 1-2 weeks of repeated exposure, with greater adaptation beyond 14 days. Five or more days already change HR and core temperature [daanen-2018-acclimation-decay]. About 2.5% of the HR and core-temperature adaptation is lost per day of decay, and re-acclimation is 8-12 times faster than decay [daanen-2018-acclimation-decay]. This is the best-graded material (A) in the topic.

### Contested or unclear

- **Size of the penalty.** Mantzios gives 0.3-0.4% per C WBGT outside 7.5-15 C for top athletes pooled across disciplines. Ely 2007 gives about 0.19% per C for top male marathoners (2.8% over about 15 C), and larger for slower runners without stating how much. Boston data suggest roughly 0.7%/C air temperature for a typical finisher against about 0.26%/C for winners [knechtle-2019-boston-weather], but that is an illustrative calculation, not a published figure.
- **Humidity.** Two marathon analyses found no independent effect once temperature is controlled [elhelou-2012-environment-marathon][vihma-2010-weather-marathon]; humidity is already inside WBGT. Humidity still matters for withdrawal risk (specific humidity) and for the sex gap in ultras [struska-2026-ultra-weather-sex].
- **Pacing shape.** Top-quartile runners show a heat-driven late deceleration, while slower runners start slower and hold it [ely-2008-pacing-temperature]. In a 161 km race everyone was slower from the start and recovered toward cool pace at night [parise-2011-161km-pacing-heat].
- **Sex.** Marathon evidence is weak and speculative [vihma-2010-weather-marathon]. A new, large ultra dataset shows men losing more speed at high air temperature [struska-2026-ultra-weather-sex]; replication is needed.
- **WBGT vs PET/UTCI as a safety screen.** WBGT underestimates risk at high radiant load [thorsson-2021-pet-vs-wbgt]; this affects warnings, not pace.
- **Pressure.** El Helou found none; Knechtle found an association.

### Unknown

- A quantitative ability multiplier, the cold-side slope, a numeric acclimation discount on the pace penalty, ultra-specific per-degree slopes, and HR-drift-based heat adjustments are not available in the abstracts. Nothing here covers heat index formulas (Steadman/Rothfusz) or the full WBGT algorithm inputs (the Liljegren inputs were recalled, not read) [liljegren-2008-wbgt-model]. Full texts were not read for any paper.

### How the engine should use this

1. Compute WBGT as the primary weather index from hourly forecast (Liljegren model, pure function in `shared/`), fall back to air temperature, and label which was used and how certain it is.
2. Apply a hot-side pace penalty of roughly 0.35% per C WBGT above 15 C for elite-level runners, plus the Ely reference table for marathons (+0.8, +1.6, +2.8% at WBGT 10-15, 15-20, 20-25 relative to 5-10 C). Multiply for slower runners with a tunable multiplier (default grade D) and calibrate against the runner's own logs.
3. Shape the pacing: uniform slower target for mid-pack and slower runners; conservative first half for fast runners.
4. For ultras, weight the penalty by hours in daytime heat, relax it at night, and show a lower-confidence label [bouscaren-2019-marathon-vs-ultra-heat][parise-2011-161km-pacing-heat].
5. When forecast WBGT is high, schedule a 7-14 day acclimation block ending close to the race, credit acclimation with 2.5%/day decay, and use a top-up block in the taper. Discount the penalty only conservatively.
6. Warn (rather than silently adjust) above WBGT 25 C, on high solar load, on large day-over-day jumps, and in the first hot days of a season.
7. Do not add an independent humidity or pressure penalty; do not apply a cold-side numeric penalty until verified.

## Gaps

- No verified source for heat index (Steadman/Rothfusz) formulas; the NWS regression is a technical report, not a paper in this corpus. WBGT from the Liljegren model has not been checked against full text or reference test values.
- No quantitative ability multiplier or per-ability slope: Ely 2007 and Vihma 2010 say slower runners lose more but the abstracts give no numbers; the full text may contain per-finisher-group figures.
- Ability-specific optimum temperatures from El Helou 2012 (quadratic model) are only in the full text.
- Cold-side slope (Mantzios 2022) and any running-specific cold pace guidance remain unverified; the ACSM cold consensus abstract carried no findings.
- No ultra-specific per-degree or WBGT-based slope for 100 km to 100 mile races; only a single 161 km two-year comparison with survivorship bias plus a 2026 ultra dataset with daily-average air temperature.
- No quantitative HR-drift, pace-per-degree or HR-cap values for heat-adjusted training sessions; Periard 2021 is a narrative review and its numeric content was not read.
- Numeric acclimation discount on the pace penalty, optimal acclimation dose for marathoners and ultra runners (versus lab trained males), and effect of passive heat (sauna, hot bath) methods are not covered.
- Evidence for age and sex effects on heat penalty is thin: Vihma 2010 and the 2026 ultra paper conflict in emphasis; no age effect verified.
- Humidity effects in ultras and warm-weather hyponatremia, hydration and cooling guidance (e.g. ice, pre-cooling) were not covered in this corpus; no verified Knechtle ultra-specific heat race analysis beyond Boston marathon; wind and precipitation effects only directional.
- Full text of every paper was not read; all findings rest on abstracts.
