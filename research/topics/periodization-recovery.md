# Periodization, recovery weeks and overreaching

## Summary for the plan engine

This topic covers periodization, recovery weeks, overreaching and, since the second pass on 2026-10-22, recovery after marathons and ultras. The second pass added 21 verified sources on post-race recovery: 1 RCT, 9 prospective cohorts, 1 retrospective cohort, 1 systematic review, 1 opinion piece and 5 coach pages. The coach pages are grade D and can set defaults but never raise a grade.

### What is established

- **Traditional periodization is the safest default.** Reverse periodization shows no advantage over traditional or block models [gonzalezrave-2022-reverse-periodization]. Elite runners use a hard day-easy day pattern with a mostly pyramidal intensity distribution [casado-2022-elite-runners-review].
- **Overreaching is a continuum, and app data cannot diagnose OTS.** FOR, NFOR and OTS are told apart only by clinical outcome and by ruling out illness, under-fuelling and iron deficiency [meeusen-2013-overtraining-consensus, weakley-2022-ots-systematic-review]. The engine may flag possible accumulated fatigue. It must not diagnose.
- **Autonomic metrics alone mislead.** HRV and HR recovery rise in overreaching as well as in positive adaptation [bellenger-2016-autonomic-hr-meta-analysis]. Subjective measures track load better than most objective ones [saw-2016-subjective-vs-objective-monitoring, roete-2021-functional-overreaching-markers]. Objective sleep efficiency drops by about 2 percentage points with overreaching [murphy-2024-overreaching-sleep].
- **Autonomic recovery after a session** takes up to 24 h after easy work, 24-48 h after threshold work and at least 48 h after high-intensity work [stanley-2013-parasympathetic-reactivation].
- **Easy running from 48 h after a marathon does not slow recovery.** In the only RCT (n = 64, mean finish 3 h 35 min), 40 min at first-threshold heart rate on days 2, 4 and 6 did not slow CK or LDH recovery. It also gave better squat-jump recovery at 96 h than rest [martinez-navarro-2021-marathon-week-rct]. Aerobic capacity was close to baseline at day 7, and soreness was no longer clearly raised from day 4 [takayama-2017-marathon-one-week]. Not everything is back by then: CK clears by about 144 h and LDH by about 192 h, but CRP is still raised at 192 h [bernat-adell-2021-marathon-biomarkers]. Jump power in elite marathoners was still 12% down on day 5 [petersen-2007-marathon-muscle-mechanics]. Easy running can come back early, but intensity should not.
- **An ultra leaves a deficit that lasts about two weeks.** After a 166 km mountain race, knee-extensor and plantar-flexor MVC fell 35-39%. Neuromuscular function was mostly back by day 9 and fully back by day 16 [millet-2011-ultramarathon-neuromuscular]. The 55 km and 118 km data show the same direction on a shorter scale [easthope-2010-trail55km-masters, martinez-navarro-2021-118km-inflammation-activity]. Muscle damage, not aerobic capacity, is the main limiter [tiller-2025-ultramarathon-muscle-damage]. Damage is largest under eccentric (downhill) load [garciavaliente-2026-trail-damage-review]. Faster finishers carry more damage markers [martinez-navarro-2021-118km-inflammation-activity, martinez-navarro-2019-118km-cardiac-hrv], and masters runners recovered more slowly after 55 km [easthope-2010-trail55km-masters].
- **Check-ins clear before function does.** After 161 km, soreness and fatigue ratings were back to pre-race levels by day 5, but 400 m speed was still 12% slower [hoffman-2017-161km-recovery-determinants]. A recovery rating that looks normal does not mean the legs are ready. HRV was still depressed 1 week after a 100-mile race and reflects autonomic state, not muscle damage [paech-2021-100mile-hrv]. Subjective recovery-stress improves roughly linearly over a month after a mountain ultra, and the same score means different things for different runners [gaudino-2019-recovery-stress-ultra].

### What is contested

- **Block versus traditional periodization.** The meta-analytic gain for block periodization is small (VO2max SMD 0.40). It comes from poor-quality studies of cyclists and skiers with no runners [molmen-2019-block-periodization-meta-analysis, issurin-2010-block-periodization]. The periodization framework itself is also criticised [kiely-2018-periodization-critique].
- **Load-ratio metrics.** No evidence supports using ACWR as a gate [impellizzeri-2020-acwr-pitfalls]. Foster's monotony and strain work only against the athlete's own thresholds [foster-1998-monotony-strain]. The link between weekly increases above 30% and injury in novices was not significant [nielsen-2014-weekly-progression].
- **How long to stop after an ultra.** Coach conventions span a wide range:
  - easy running the next day for experienced ultrarunners and up to three weeks off for newcomers [irunfar-2023-torrence-post-ultra-downtime];
  - runs of no more than 20 min in week 1 [cts-2025-koop-ultra-recovery];
  - 25-45 min jogs in week 1 [irunfar-2016-powell-post-100-return];
  - a week of rest, then three weeks of graded easy running [skurka-2017-roche-100-recovery];
  - one day off per 10 miles raced, or per 10 km on mountain courses [irunfar-2014-uhan-ultra-recovery-rules].
    None of these pages cites data.
- **Whether recovery scales with distance.** A 330 km race run at slow pace caused less damage than a 166 km race [saugy-2013-330km-neuromuscular]. Running on stacked days prolonged peripheral impairment compared with a single stage [besson-2020-single-vs-multistage-ultra].

### What is unknown

- No study compares 3:1 with 2:1 recovery cycles, or tests how deep a cutback should be, in runners. The 3:1 cycle and the 20-30% cut are convention (grade D).
- No trial tests any return-to-running protocol after 50 km or longer. The marathon RCT does not extend to ultras. What running load is safe in the first 9 days after an ultra is unknown.
- No study tests whether gating the return on check-ins or wearable data changes outcomes.
- Nothing in this corpus covers recovery after a 5 km to half-marathon race.
- Immune risk: no post-marathon "open window" was found. Runners who were ill before the race had about twice the post-race infection rate (33% vs 16%) [ekblom-2006-infection-marathon]. No ultra data exist.
- Age, sex and menstrual-cycle effects are thinly covered. Easthope's masters result is the only age-specific post-race data. Free-living wearable thresholds are not validated.

### How the engine should use this

1. Default to base, build, specific and taper, with a 3:1 build-to-recovery cycle. Use 2:1 for novices, post-injury runners and while a flag is active. Both are defaults.
2. Space quality sessions at least 48 h after high-intensity work and 24-48 h after threshold work.
3. Insert an unplanned recovery week only when two independent signals hold for about 7 days. A single signal trims the next hard session. HRV, VO2max estimates and ACWR never act alone.
4. Use three fatigue states and never diagnose OTS.
5. **Count the post-race window in days from the race, not in plan weeks.** It has three stages: a hold with no running, then 7-day steps of easy running, then the normal plan, restarted through J7.
6. **Hold lengths:** marathon 2 days (grade B), 50 km class 4, 100 km class 7, 100 miles 10, beyond 14 (grade D). Multiply the hold by 1.5, rounded up, when the course descends at least 30 m/km, the runner is 40 or older, or it is the runner's first finish at that distance class. Apply the multiplier once, not once per reason. Walking and light cross-training are allowed during the hold.
7. **Steps:** 0.5 then 0.75 of the pre-taper reference week for the marathon and 50 km classes; 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 for 100 km and longer.
   - First step: at most 3 runs, on non-consecutive days, each 40 min or less, in Z1.
   - Second step: runs of 60 min or less.
   - Later steps: the longest run is 90 min or less.
8. **No intervals, threshold, race pace, strides, plyometrics or downhill sessions until the steps end.**
9. **Check-ins can only delay.** A step advances only when the last 2 check-ins before it are not poor, by the J2 rule or against the runner's own pre-race median, and report no pain or illness. Otherwise the current stage extends one day at a time. With no ratings, the default schedule runs, and the app says that ratings are needed for it to adjust.
10. HRV and resting HR can delay a stage through J3 and J4 but never advance one. A completed race of marathon distance or longer (42.195 km or more) does not count toward the 30-day reference, in the check and in the generator alike; shorter races count as runs.

## Gaps

- No study compares 3:1 versus 2:1 build:recovery cycles, or any recovery-week magnitude, in runners; all values are convention (grade D).
- No trial tests any return-to-running protocol after a 50 km or longer race; the only RCT is a marathon (40 min x 3 at VT1 from 48 h). What running load is safe in the first 9 days after an ultra is unknown, so the ultra holds and step fractions rest on time-course cohorts plus coach convention.
- Ultra recovery time courses have coarse sampling (Millet: days 2, 5, 9, 16; Hoffman: speed to day 5 only; Martinez-Navarro 118 km: days 3 and 7; Paech: one 1-week point). No day-by-day data exist between day 9 and day 16, and none after 100 miles beyond Saugy's immediate post-race values.
- No evidence on recovery after a 5 km, 10 km or half-marathon race; the existing single 0.5 week is an unsupported default.
- No study tests whether gating a post-race return on check-ins or wearable data changes injury, illness or performance outcomes.
- No HRV or resting-HR normalisation time is known after any ultra; morning wearable readings were not studied (Paech used 24 h Holter).
- Effort and pace modify damage (faster finishers carry more), but the engine has no validated effort input; race RPE is a possible proxy, untested.
- Women are underrepresented in every post-race cohort; masters evidence is one 55 km study with no 72 h values in the abstract.
- No immune data after ultras; Ekblom covers marathons by self-report only.
- No evidence on when strength training should resume after a race.
- Block periodization evidence is from cyclists and skiers with low methodological quality; no running or ultra-distance trials.
- No validated free-living thresholds for wearable overreaching detection; weekly volume progression evidence is exploratory and restricted to novices.
