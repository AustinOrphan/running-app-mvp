# Periodization, recovery weeks and overreaching

## Summary for the plan engine

All 14 researcher entries resolved in Crossref with matching title, journal, year and first author, and every finding was re-checked against the PubMed abstract. No entry was dropped. Corrections: unsupported phrases were removed (Roete "mostly cycling", Nuuttila "non-randomized", Bellenger "authors flag methodological issues"), Foster was reclassified as `other` design, and several evidence levels were downgraded where primary-study quality was poor (Mølmen, González-Ravé, Casado, Roete, Murphy). Three papers were added: Issurin 2010, the IOC load consensus, and Nielsen 2014 on weekly distance progression.

### What is established

- **Traditional periodization is the safest default.** Reverse periodization shows no performance advantage over traditional or block models [gonzalezrave-2022-reverse-periodization]. Elite runners follow a hard day-easy day pattern, with a mostly pyramidal intensity distribution and marathoners leaning more pyramidal than 1500 m runners [casado-2022-elite-runners-review].
- **Overreaching is a continuum, and OTS cannot be diagnosed from app data.** Functional overreaching, NFOR and OTS are separated only by clinical outcome and by excluding illness, under-fuelling, and iron or magnesium deficiency [meeusen-2013-overtraining-consensus]. A systematic review found zero studies with objective pre-to-OTS data and more than 4 weeks of suppressed performance [weakley-2022-ots-systematic-review]. The engine may flag possible accumulated fatigue and suggest a medical review. It must not diagnose.
- **Autonomic metrics alone are misleading.** HRV and HR recovery rise in overreaching as well as in positive adaptation [bellenger-2016-autonomic-hr-meta-analysis]. HRV did not reflect functional overreaching, while submaximal efficiency, RPE and performance did [roete-2021-functional-overreaching-markers]. Subjective measures track load more sensitively than most objective ones, and the two often disagree [saw-2016-subjective-vs-objective-monitoring]. Objective sleep efficiency falls with overreaching (about -2 percentage points), though perceived sleep quality does not [murphy-2024-overreaching-sleep].
- **Recovery after hard sessions takes time.** Autonomic recovery takes up to 24 h after easy work, 24-48 h after threshold work and at least 48 h after high-intensity work, and it is faster in fitter athletes [stanley-2013-parasympathetic-reactivation]. This covers one recovery system only, not muscle or glycogen.

### What is contested

- **Block versus traditional.** Meta-analytic effects favour block periodization slightly (VO2max SMD 0.40, CI 0.02-0.79), but primary studies are poor (PEDro 3.7/10) and the evidence comes from cyclists and skiers. No runners appear in the full text [molmen-2019-block-periodization-meta-analysis]. The conceptual case is in [issurin-2010-block-periodization].
- **Fixed templates versus adaptive planning.** The periodization framework itself is criticised [kiely-2018-periodization-critique], but no alternative has comparative evidence.
- **Load-ratio metrics.** Impellizzeri et al. find no evidence supporting ACWR for load management [impellizzeri-2020-acwr-pitfalls]. Foster's monotony and strain were tied to illness using individual thresholds only [foster-1998-monotony-strain]. A progression of more than 30% per week was associated with more distance-related injuries in novices, but not significantly (HR 1.59, 95% CI 0.96-2.66) [nielsen-2014-weekly-progression].

### What is unknown

- **No study compares 3:1 with 2:1 recovery-week cycles**, or any cutback magnitude, in runners. The searches found none. The 3:1 convention and a 20-30% volume reduction are coaching practice, not evidence, and must be labelled grade D.
- **Recovery time after long runs and ultras** is not covered by any source found. This matters for 100-mile goals.
- **Age, sex and menstrual-cycle effects** on overreaching and recovery spacing are not covered by this corpus.
- **Free-living wearable thresholds** for overreaching (efficiency drop, sleep drop) are not validated. Everything cited comes from controlled overload protocols.

### How the engine should use this

1. Default to a traditional base, build, specific, taper sequence with 3 build weeks and 1 recovery week. Make 2:1 the setting for novices, masters, returning-from-injury athletes and active flags. Treat both as defaults, not findings.
2. Space quality sessions by at least 48 h after high-intensity work and 24-48 h after threshold work. Place the long run on a day that does not sit between two quality days, as a design choice.
3. Make unplanned recovery-week insertion adaptive. Require two independent signals (subjective decline, submaximal efficiency decline, sleep-efficiency drop) sustained over several days. A single signal only trims the next hard session.
4. Never use resting HRV, VO2max estimates or ACWR as the sole trigger. Display monotony and strain descriptively against the athlete's own history.
5. Use three states (normal, possible accumulated fatigue, persistent suppression). Persistent suppression leads to a medical-review prompt that mentions illness, under-fuelling and iron. Never output an OTS or NFOR diagnosis.
6. Cap week-to-week volume increases conservatively and make no injury-prevention claims.
7. Mark block mode and reverse sequencing as optional and non-default.

See the rules and gaps below for parameter values.

## Gaps

- No study found comparing 3:1 versus 2:1 build:recovery cycles, or any recovery-week magnitude, in runners; all values are convention (grade D).
- No evidence on recovery time after long runs, back-to-back long runs, marathons or ultra efforts; the Stanley autonomic spacing covers single aerobic sessions only and not muscular or glycogen recovery.
- Block periodization evidence is from cyclists and skiers with low methodological quality; no running or ultra-distance trials. Reverse periodization has few running studies.
- No validated free-living thresholds for wearable overreaching detection (efficiency, sleep, HR acceleration); evidence comes from controlled overload protocols in mostly non-running athletes.
- Age, sex, menstrual-cycle and masters-athlete effects on overreaching and recovery spacing are not covered by this corpus.
- Weekly volume progression evidence is exploratory and restricted to novices; none for experienced or ultra-distance runners, where vertical gain, time-on-feet and back-to-back long runs matter.
- Searches did not locate pre-2024 running-specific trials of recovery-week insertion or of marathon-recovery time course; a targeted search for these (and 2025-2026 work) is still worth running.
