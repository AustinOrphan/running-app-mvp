# Terrain, gradient and elevation

## What is established

**Energy cost versus gradient.** Level running costs 3.40 J/kg/m independent of speed; cost rises steeply on climbs (18.93 J/kg/m at +45% gradient, about 5.6 times level) and falls on descents to a minimum near -20% gradient (1.73 J/kg/m, about half of level) before rising again (3.92 J/kg/m at -45%) [minetti-2002-gradient-cost]. A narrative review reports the same -20% hinge [vernillo-2017-graded-running-review]. This is the only quantitative base for a grade-adjusted pace (GAP) model in the corpus; it comes from 10 runners on a treadmill, so it is a population prior, not a personal law.

**Walking beats running above a grade that depends on climbing rate.** At a fixed 800 m/h of climbing, trained runners chose to walk above about 7.2 degrees (about 12.6% grade), and the energetic cost crossover, not heart rate or mechanical work, matched that choice [finiel-2026-uphill-walk-run]. At 1,260 m/h, walking was cheaper than running on every slope of 15.8 degrees (about 28%) and steeper [giovanelli-2016-vk-energetics]; at 30 degrees walking cost less below about 0.7 m/s and the costs met at 0.8 m/s [ortiz-2017-30deg-walk-run]. Earlier work puts the walk/run transition speed falling about 1% per 1% incline [hubel-2013-walk-run-incline].

**Downhill damage and protection.** Unaccustomed downhill running causes muscle damage that impairs later performance; slope, duration and speed are the main drivers, and prior downhill exposure is the best-supported protection [bontemps-2020-downhill-review]. A single 30 min bout at -20% grade protected against strength loss, pain and biomechanical decline when repeated 3 weeks later [khassetarash-2023-rbe-downhill], consistent with the repeated bout effect whose mechanism is unresolved [mchugh-2003-repeated-bout]. After a mountain ultramarathon, downhill energy cost rose 13.1% while level and uphill cost did not change [vernillo-2015-fatigue-energy-cost]; in race data, runners slow more on descents than ascents late in ultras [genitrini-2022-downhill-pacing].

## What is contested

- **Shape of the uphill cost curve.** One review calls it linear in slope [vernillo-2017-graded-running-review]; the Minetti abstract gives anchor values only, so the functional form must come from the full text.
- **Optimal climbing angle.** Minetti puts the optimum for mountain paths at 20-30% gradient; Giovanelli finds broad minima of vertical cost at 20.4-35.0 degrees (about 37-70% grade) at a fixed vertical speed [giovanelli-2016-vk-energetics]. The definitions differ, so these are not directly comparable.
- **Downhill training value.** Five weeks of -10% running improved knee-extension torque 9-24% but not aerobic capacity in 18 young men; the same study's level group improved VO2max [toyomura-2018-downhill-training]. Whether this transfers to ultra performance is untested.
- **What limits climbing late in an ultra.** Authors infer knee-extensor fatigue rather than oxygen delivery from correlations [jeker-2020-vertical-speed-ultra]; this is an inference.

## What is unknown

No source in the corpus quantifies trail technicality penalties, race-average vertical gain per hour for 50 km to 100 mile events, durable GAP at ultra distances, how long repeated-bout protection lasts, or the dose-response of downhill conditioning in recreational runners. The classic road endurance model predicts trail performance less well [dewaal-2021-trail-physiology-sr], so trail predictions deserve wider intervals.

## How the engine should use it

1. **Grade-adjusted pace.** Use the Minetti cost ratio (cost at gradient divided by 3.40 J/kg/m) as the default effort-equivalence curve, and personalise it from the runner's own graded efforts. On descents, apply the metabolic credit only down to about -20%, and do not turn it into a pace credit without a cap: the paper reports estimated descent speeds well above those reached in races [minetti-2002-gradient-cost]. Add a late-race downhill fade (about +13% cost in the one measurement) for mountain ultras [vernillo-2015-fatigue-energy-cost]. Treat all numbers as priors with wide intervals.
2. **Hiking versus running.** Make the switch a function of grade and climbing rate. Defaults: walk above about 12.6% grade at about 800 m/h, above about 28% grade at about 1,260 m/h, and by default on grades near 30 degrees (about 58%). Tune the switch with the runner's data; use a cost or power criterion, not heart rate [finiel-2026-uphill-walk-run]. For sustained ultra climbing, the only reference is a 547 m/h mean uphill vertical speed (SD 135) from 27 runners in one 220 km race, falling with distance [jeker-2020-vertical-speed-ultra].
3. **Intensity versus vertical speed.** Prescribe climbing by effort or power, because the same effort gave different ascent speeds on 10% and 25% slopes [hingrand-2023-power-vs-ascent-speed]. A single m/h target is a planning figure for a course, not an intensity.
4. **Downhill conditioning.** Treat unaccustomed descents near the research protocol (about -12% slope, 40 min, 11 km/h) as high-damage load and introduce them gradually, with a first moderate exposure about 3 weeks before heavy downhill work or a downhill-heavy race [bontemps-2020-downhill-review, khassetarash-2023-rbe-downhill]. Track descent volume separately from ascent. Use downhill running for strength specificity, not aerobic development [toyomura-2018-downhill-training].
5. **Vert targets.** Derive weekly and race-specific vertical-gain targets from the course (distance, ascent, descent, altitude, surface, as the reporting statement recommends [scheer-2020-defining-off-road-running]) and cap progression of descent volume more tightly than ascent.
6. **Altitude.** For acute exposure above roughly 800 m expect maximal aerobic capacity to fall about 6.3% per 1,000 m (range 4.6-7.5%) [wehrlin-2006-altitude-linear]; label altitude pace adjustments approximate and exclude acclimatisation.

## Gaps

- No paper in the corpus gives the full cost-of-running versus gradient equation; the Minetti coefficients must be fetched from the full text before implementing GAP.
- No validation of a Minetti-based grade-adjusted pace model against real recreational trail and ultra race or training data, nor of how much it varies by runner.
- No quantification of trail technicality (rocks, roots, mud, snow) as a pace or cost multiplier; the off-road definition statement only recommends reporting.
- No source for race-average vertical gain per hour across 50 km to 100 mile events, or for how vert/h scales with distance, fitness and total ascent.
- Hiking evidence uses short bouts in trained runners; none covers fatigued power hiking, poles, or walking durability over many hours.
- Repeated bout effect: protection duration, minimum effective dose and interaction with training load are not established for runners; evidence comes from small, young, mostly male samples.
- No sex-, age- or training-status-specific gradient or downhill parameters; samples are mostly young and male.
- Downhill durability: no trial testing whether a downhill conditioning block improves mountain ultra performance or reduces late-race fade.
- Altitude: only acute-exposure laboratory data; no acclimatisation, altitude-training or race-pace translation, and no data above 2,800 m.
