# Training methods

## Summary for the plan engine

This topic holds what named training methods prescribe, as read in a source, and what the corpus says about choosing one for a runner. It was written by hand on 2026-10-31 for task M1 of `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-31-sources-methods-evaluation-proposal.md` (sections 4.1, 4.2 and 4.6; decisions 5, 6 and 13), not by the corpus workflow: a method is described from a source that was read, and a model's summary is never a source. Each candidate of sections 4.1 and 4.2 was located by DOI or URL on 2026-10-31. Six were read in full text (Casado 2023, Festa 2019, Boullosa 2020, Meixner 2025, Qin 2025 and the already recorded González-Ravé 2022) and three by abstract only because the full text is closed access (Seiler 2010, Zinner 2018, Scudamore 2018); two commentaries (Burnley 2022 and Foster 2022) have no abstract and were not read. Each record says which. Two programme pages that a preset would credit were read in full and recorded at grade D. The book of each method is task S3's: no book was read here, and no book is recorded from a page about it.

Two points carry the rest. First, no study compares the plans of these methods as packages, and none shows that matching a method to a characteristic of the runner gives better races, so every preset of section (p) of `engine-rules.md` is grade D, a choice and not a better plan. Second, the one trial that reports such a match (Qin 2025) was read in full and is not admitted; its account is in the section below.

## What the records support

- **No method has been tested as a package.** The one study of published marathon plans, 92 non-peer-reviewed 12-week plans, states there is no evidence for the effectiveness of the plans it analysed [knopp-2024-marathon-plans]. The pages read here describe a method and give no outcome data: the FIRST page names its studies and gives no result [furman-2023-first-three-quality-runs], and the Foundation's principles page is a promoter's account of a method of the 1950s and 1960s [lydiardfoundation-2026-lydiard-principles].
- **Most sessions are easy.** The description of competitive athletes, about 80% of sessions at low intensity with high-intensity work through the cycle, comes from one narrative review, read by abstract [seiler-2010-best-practice-tid]; the time-based evidence behind the E4 floor is in the topic intensity-distribution.
- **Base first is the order of the default, and nothing shows it beats another order.** The traditional order is the engine's E1 [casado-2022-elite-runners-review]; a systematic review of reverse periodization (re-read in full for M1) finds it no more effective than traditional or block periodization, with 11 studies of 200 or 230 athletes, three of them in running, mostly short and of low to moderate quality [gonzalezrave-2022-reverse-periodization]. The Lydiard Foundation page states the same order and gives no base length [lydiardfoundation-2026-lydiard-principles].
- **Recreational runners have little direct evidence.** The review that states "no consensus on the best practice" for recreational runners in 2020 says that the intensity-distribution literature in them is scarce, then recommends a polarized distribution and reverse periodization for marathons from the authors' experience; the second conflicts with the systematic review above, and its account of one trial is stronger than that trial's primary result [boullosa-2020-recreational-runners-review, munoz-2014-polarized-recreational].
- **Equal gains were common in short trials of recreational runners.** An 8-week randomised trial of 38 runners found a polarized four-session plan and a focused three-session plan, matched on training impulse, gave similar gains, with 17% less time for the focused plan; the contrast is intensity and frequency together [festa-2019-polarized-vs-focused-recreational]. An 8-week study of 42 runners found a low-intensity block gave more responders and an economy gain [zinner-2018-tid-mesocycles-recreational]. The pooled evidence finds no overall difference [rosenblat-2025-tid-ipd-nma].

## Contested

- **Fewer run days.** Direction only: three days a week had fewer injuries than five in young men jogging hard [pollock-1977-frequency-duration-injury], a trend against three days for novices against two [kluitenberg-2016-nlstart2run-training-factors], and no relation between frequency and marathon time at equal monthly volume in men in one race [yamaguchi-2023-volume-frequency-run-distance]. Across marathon cohorts, more weekly runs coincided with faster finishes in univariate models [doherty-2020-marathon-training-meta]. No trial holds volume equal and varies run days in recreational runners over 5 km to the half marathon, the goals for which P2 is offered.
- **A threshold-heavy double day.** The Norwegian model is described by its authors with no controlled study and only in 1500 m and 5000 m runners [casado-2023-lactate-guided-threshold]; two training sessions a day went with injury in trail runners [viljoen-2022-trail-risk-factors]. The engine offers neither.
- **VDOT paces.** The calculator's threshold pace agreed with laboratory threshold pace in recreational runners and its interval pace was slower than pace at VO2max, in 20 runners [scudamore-2018-vdot-paces-evaluation]. The engine anchors paces on thresholds (C2).
- **Polarized against pyramidal.** A published dispute exists (Burnley, Bearden and Jones 2022 against Foster, Casado, Esteve-Lanao, Haugen and Seiler 2022, both in Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise 54(6)). Neither commentary has an abstract in Europe PMC or PubMed and both are closed access, so neither was read in M1 and neither is recorded.

## Does a characteristic of the runner predict the better method?

As the corpus now holds it: no. The pieces are these.

- **Level.** The one exploratory subgroup of the network meta-analysis (competitive athletes responded better to polarized, recreational athletes to pyramidal, on VO2max in 13 short studies) is the only pooled signal, and the overall comparison is null [rosenblat-2025-tid-ipd-nma]; the polarized edge on VO2peak is in blocks under 12 weeks and in highly trained athletes, and not in time trials [oliveira-2024-pol-meta]; elite marathoners lean pyramidal and 1500 m runners polarized, descriptively [casado-2022-elite-runners-review].
- **Response.** One block cannot separate response from noise [hecksteden-2015-individual-response], and G4 forbids responder labels. The two trials that count responders from one block each show a direction at most: more responders with a low-intensity block in one study [zinner-2018-tid-mesocycles-recreational], and equal gains from two distributions in another [festa-2019-polarized-vs-focused-recreational]. A pooled analysis of adapting training day to day found submaximal markers improved and performance did not [duking-2021-hrv-guided-meta].
- **Injury history and days free.** Injury history raises risk [vanpoppel-2021-overuse-risk-factors-review], a reason for G-3 and not a method; novices who stopped within 26 weeks were 29.5% of 774 [fokkema-2019-discontinuation-start-to-run].
- **No consensus.** A review of recreational runners says so [boullosa-2020-recreational-runners-review].

## Read and not admitted: Qin 2025

Qin G, Lee S and Kim S, "Machine learning-based personalized training models for optimizing marathon performance through pyramidal and polarized training intensity distributions", Scientific Reports 15:41516 (2025), doi 10.1038/s41598-025-25369-7, open access, received 2025-05-08, accepted 2025-10-20. The full text, Tables 1 to 7 and Figures 1, 3, 7 and 9 were read on 2026-10-31. Decision 13 admits it at its design level if R1 to R6 pass. They do not pass, and no record is written; the reasons are these.

**What it reports.** A parallel-group trial: 120 recreational and semi-elite marathon runners (68 men, 52 women; 116 completed) were randomly assigned, stratified by sex, age group and experience, to a 16-week pyramidal (70/20/10% of time in zones 1, 2 and 3) or polarized (80/5/15%) programme before an autumn marathon. Marathon time improved by 11.3 ± 3.2 minutes with the polarized programme and 8.7 ± 2.8 minutes with the pyramidal (p < 0.03, d = 0.81), with 17.3% less volume in the polarized arm. A clustering of individual responses gave four groups (polarized responders 31.5%, pyramidal responders 31.9%, dual responders 18.7%, non-responders 17.9%), and the abstract reads a correlation of r = 0.72 as experience predicting the better method.

**What it supports.** Nothing the engine can use. Both arms improved their marathon time (every runner in Figure 7a improved by about 4 minutes or more), in a 16-week block. It is one trial of one population at one race.

**What it does not support, from its own text.**

- The training is not described. The Methods give no weekly volume, session content, zone definitions for the prescription, supervision or measure of the distribution achieved; the 70/20/10 and 80/5/15 figures appear only in the Introduction and Figure 3. The polarized arm trained 17.3% less, so the arms differ in volume as well as distribution.
- No registration is named. The article names no registry, trial identifier or CONSORT flow, no blinding of outcome assessors and no sample-size calculation for the primary outcome (the one it gives is a ratio of observations to features for the models). Searches of ClinicalTrials.gov (three query sets) and ISRCTN returned no study sponsored by Hanyang University or by these authors (six other registered trials of intensity distribution appeared, by other sponsors, and were not read); other registries were not searched.
- The dates are not given and do not fit easily. The paper was received on 2025-05-08 and describes a 16-week trial before an autumn marathon, and its ethics approval is numbered HYU-2025-001. If the 2025 in that number is the year of approval, a trial that ended in an autumn marathon before 2025-05-08 must have been run in 2024, before the approval; if the marathon was in autumn 2025, the paper was received before it. The text gives no trial dates, so this is a question for the authors and not a finding.
- The reported statistics do not follow from each other. Table 6's between-group p-values (for example 0.042 for VO2max, 0.018 for leg stiffness) cannot be reproduced from its means and standard deviations at about 58 per arm: a two-sample test of the printed values gives p below 0.0001 for each, and d = 0.86 for the marathon outcome against the 0.81 printed (recomputed 2026-10-31, an assumption of 58 to 60 per arm because the arm counts of completers are not given). The cluster shares of 31.5, 31.9, 18.7 and 17.9% are not whole-number shares of 116 or 120 runners. The text says "As shown in Table 1" that the arms were similar at baseline, but Table 1 shows men against women and no arm comparison. The lactate-threshold velocity gains are given in km/h in the text (+9.3 and +6.7), and Table 6 lists them under a "% change" heading; the two units cannot both hold.
- The responder and matching analyses cannot be what the abstract says. In a parallel-group design each runner trained under one programme, yet Figure 9a plots each runner with a pyramidal and a polarized improvement, and draws the non-responder boundary at 7% on both axes, a threshold and not a typical error. The correlation r = 0.72 is reported as experience against pyramidal effectiveness, with r = 0.68 against polarized effectiveness, in the Results, while Table 7 has the pyramidal gain falling with experience (12.4% for novices to 5.6% for the most experienced) and the polarized gain rising (5.8% to 13.7%), the opposite sign for the pyramidal arm. The machine-learning models' accuracy is reported for predicting marathon time and classifying zones (Table 5), not for choosing the better method. Table 4 lists neural-network hyperparameters (dropout, epochs, hidden units) for models that the Methods call gradient boosting and a support vector machine.
- Its percentages of improvement (about 5 to 14% in Table 7 and Figures 9 and 10) cannot be percentage improvements in marathon time. Weighted by the sizes of the four experience groups, Table 7 gives 8.8% for the pyramidal arm and 9.4% for the polarized arm, against 3.4% and 4.5% of the mean personal best (253.6 minutes) for the 8.7 and 11.3 minutes of the headline, and the text does not say what they are.
- The authors' names and affiliations (Hanyang University, Seoul) are the paper's own; searches of the web and of OpenAlex found no record independent of them. The data are available on request only.

**Conflict with the corpus.** The headline, polarized training gaining more on less volume, conflicts with the pooled null of the network meta-analysis [rosenblat-2025-tid-ipd-nma], with the no-difference result for time trials [oliveira-2024-pol-meta] and with the recreational-runner trials above [festa-2019-polarized-vs-focused-recreational, munoz-2014-polarized-recreational]. The matching claim has no second source. Gate results: R1 pass (read in full, with its tables and four figures); R2 pass on the standard of the other journal records of this session (named authors and the affiliations of the publisher deposit; no independent record was found); R3 pass (published 2025-11-24, no Crossref notice); R4 hold (a single trial, contradicted by a graded paper, with no second source for the matching claim); R5 hold (the training and the runners' volumes are not described well enough to say what use would be an extrapolation); R6 not applicable.

**What would change this.** A registration or a protocol, the training logs or the data, a correction that reconciles the tables with the text, or a replication. Until then no rule, Param, preset or topic row relies on it, and it stays out of the corpus; the owner may still ask for a record at level D with R4 and R5 on hold, cited by nothing.

## What is unknown

- No trial compares the plans of two named methods in recreational runners over 5 km to the marathon, with the training verified and the outcome a race.
- No study varies the number of run days at equal volume in recreational runners over 5 km to the half marathon.
- No study tests the length of the base phase, or strides-only base work, in recreational runners.
- No controlled study of the lactate-guided threshold model, and none outside 1500 m and 5000 m runners.
- No source read here gives a prescription from a coaching book. The books (Daniels, Pfitzinger, Hansons, Koop, Uphill Athlete, Hudson, Lydiard, Pierce, Murr and Moss) are task S3's, read from pages the owner holds.

## How the engine should use this

1. A preset is a named choice among a few structural values, shown as a choice and not as a better plan. The rules are P1 to P4 in section (p) of `engine-rules.md`: P1 the default, P2 three run days for 5 km to half-marathon goals, P3 not offered, P4 a longer base.
2. Nothing here selects a method for a runner. A fit note may state a fact the page already loads (the days a runner has, the goal's class) and never a score; nothing is inferred from logs (G3, G4), and a language model never picks (ADR 0005).
3. A source is credited to a preset only once its record passes R1 and R2. The two pages credited here are programme pages at grade D; a book is credited only after task S3 reads its pages.

## Gaps

- The pages the proposal names for Pfitzinger and the Hansons plan were not re-read in M1, and the Koop, Uphill Athlete and Maffetone pages refused a plain fetch in the proposal's survey and were not tried again; none is credited by a preset.
- Seiler 2010, Zinner 2018 and Scudamore 2018 are recorded from their abstracts; their full texts are closed access.
- The two 2022 commentaries on polarized training and the two 2019 comments on Zinner 2018 are closed access with no abstract and were not read.
