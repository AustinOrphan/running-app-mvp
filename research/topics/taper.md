# Tapering

## Tapering: what the engine should do

All 13 researcher entries resolved in Crossref and matched title, journal, year and first author. Their abstracts were re-read in Europe PMC and none were dropped. Several claims went beyond the abstract and were corrected. The most important correction is that [smyth-2021-marathon-taper-big-data] does not state that tapers longer than 3 weeks give no extra benefit or cause detraining. Two papers were added: [hug-2014-taper-marathon-hrv] for wearable-relevant taper physiology and [bussau-2002-carb-loading-one-day] for the pre-race week.

### Well established

- **Taper length.** A taper of about 2 weeks is the best-supported general default. The meta-analyses support a window of 7-21 days [bosquet-2007-taper-meta-analysis, wang-2023-taper-endurance-sr-ma]. The marathon big-data cohort found tapers of up to 3 weeks associated with better results than shorter ones [smyth-2021-marathon-taper-big-data].
- **Volume.** Reduce it 41-60% on average. Taper-length findings come from the same meta-analyses [bosquet-2007-taper-meta-analysis, wang-2023-taper-endurance-sr-ma]. Elite marathoners reduce continuous volume to about 53% of regular [spilsbury-2015-elite-runner-taper-survey].
- **Intensity and frequency.** Keep intensity and frequency essentially unchanged. Mujika & Padilla cap any frequency cut at 20% [mujika-2003-taper-scientific-bases, mujika-2010-intense-training-taper].
- **Pattern.** Progressive is at least as good as step and sometimes better [mujika-2003-taper-scientific-bases, thomas-2005-taper-model-simulation, wang-2023-taper-endurance-sr-ma].
- **What a taper does.** It dissipates fatigue. It does not raise VO2max or economy on average [wang-2023-taper-endurance-sr-ma, mujika-2004-taper-physiology].

### Contested or weakly supported

- **Maximum duration.** Mujika's 4 to more than 28 days is too wide to use. The 3-week cap in the cohort and in Aubry's finding that peaks arrive in the first two taper weeks is the practical ceiling for recreational runners, but neither source tests 4 weeks directly [aubry-2014-overreaching-before-taper].
- **Overload before taper.** The meta-analysis and the model favour it. Aubry found the benefit disappears if the athlete becomes functionally overreached, and infections were 70% in that subgroup. That study used male triathletes, not runners [aubry-2014-overreaching-before-taper, wang-2023-taper-endurance-sr-ma, thomas-2005-taper-model-simulation].
- **Step versus progressive.** The pooled data allow both. The cohort associates strict (progressively decreasing) weekly volume with better marathons, but this is observational and runners who taper strictly may differ in other ways.
- **Taper size.** The 41-60% band is a pooled average. Single trials span 50-85% cuts with mixed results. Several are 6-7 day studies in 8-24 athletes [houmard-1994-taper-distance-runners, mujika-2000-taper-volume-null-result, mujika-2002-taper-frequency-middle-distance].
- **Sex.** Women show larger finish-time associations than men in the cohort. This is an association only, so no sex parameter is justified.
- **Monitoring markers.** Resting heart rate should be stable in healthy tapering athletes [mujika-2004-taper-physiology]. In nine marathoners, post-exercise RMSSD fell in taper week 2 while performance rose, and it did not predict the gain [hug-2014-taper-marathon-hrv]. HRV alerts must therefore not be triggered by a drop alone.

### Unknown

- **Ultramarathons.** There is no peer-reviewed taper study in a 50 km to 100-mile population that could be found in Europe PMC, PubMed or a web search. Everything for ultras is extrapolation from marathon and middle-distance data plus coaching consensus.
- **Heat, altitude and travel.** These are flagged as unresolved [pyne-2009-peaking-limitations].
- **Taper-by-age and taper-by-fitness interactions.** Nothing in the corpus tests them directly.
- **Recreational randomized trials.** There are none in the corpus. The only recreational evidence is observational [smyth-2021-marathon-taper-big-data].
- **The last two days before a race.** No study tests a run on the day before a race, or the last day for a quality session, against any outcome (see below).

### The last two days before a long race (2026-11-01, task RF3)

Written before rule K6 was given numbers for the days before a race of 90 minutes or more, from the corpus and from two online searches on 2026-11-01 (Europe PMC and Crossref, on a shake-out, a run the day before a race and the final days of a marathon or ultra taper), which found no study of a run on those days and one protocol that fixes the running of its last days.

What the corpus holds. One day of a high-carbohydrate diet (10 g per kg a day) in eight men who stayed physically inactive raised muscle glycogen to the level of three days, so the final 24-48 hours need no running and no depletion phase [bussau-2002-carb-loading-one-day]. The one protocol that tapered the running as well ran a 5-day sequence of 90, 40, 40, 20 and 20 min before a 20.9-km run, with a rest day between the last run and the test, and found that the loading raised the glycogen and did not change the run time of trained runners [sherman-1981-exercise-diet-glycogen]. The reviews put the volume cut of a taper at 41-60% on average and up to 60-90% in the review of Mujika and Padilla, and keep intensity and frequency, a cut of no more than 20%, so the final days are the low end of a taper that keeps its run days and not a stop of it [bosquet-2007-taper-meta-analysis, mujika-2003-taper-scientific-bases]. Muscle glycogen rises progressively across a taper, the markers of muscle damage fall, and mood and sleep improve [mujika-2004-taper-physiology]. Elite marathoners cut their continuous volume to about 53% of regular, which says nothing of the last days singly [spilsbury-2015-elite-runner-taper-survey].

What it does not hold. No study compares a short run, a rest day and no change on the day before a race, or says on which day the last quality session or the last long run should fall. The carbohydrate studies measured glycogen with the athletes inactive or resting before the test, in eight men and in runners of unstated number, and neither measured the time of a race of 90 minutes or more; the taper reviews say nothing of single days. The size of a shake-out and the number of easy days are therefore a design default (grade D) and not a finding: a cap of 20 min is the length of the last two runs of the one protocol, and that protocol rested on the day before the test, so a rest day (a cap of 0) is as well supported as a short run. A rest day the day before is also the more conservative choice (G6); a run is kept only so that the taper keeps its run days (K3).

### How the engine should use it

1. Choose taper length from race distance and pre-taper load: 7-10 days for 5-10 km, 14 days for half-marathon and 21 days for marathon. Cap at 21 days. Long, heavy build-ups may go to the cap and light builds should use the lower end.
2. Express each taper week as a multiplier of the pre-taper peak or average week, strictly decreasing. The mean multiplier over the taper falls in 0.40-0.59.
3. Keep every scheduled run day, with at most a 20% reduction in sessions. Shorten runs first. Keep one quality session per week at the pre-taper quality pace, with fewer repetitions.
4. Validate plans, flagging any taper that is flat, increasing or longer than the cap.
5. Show benefits conservatively, as a sketch: about 0.5-3%, with the marathon cohort's 2.6% median presented as an association, never a promise.
6. Add a taper-phase check-in for illness, niggles and sleep. A concern should prompt rest or a lighter week, never a streak or "behind" message. Use wearable HR and HRV only as corroborating signals.
7. For the final 24-48 hours before races over about 90 minutes, include rest and high-carbohydrate guidance [bussau-2002-carb-loading-one-day]. For ultras and heat, state that taper content is extrapolated and give a conservative default with override.

## Gaps

- No peer-reviewed taper study in ultramarathon or trail-running populations (50 km to 100+ miles) was found in Europe PMC, PubMed or a web search; ultra rules are extrapolated and graded D.
- No randomized controlled trials of tapering in recreational runners; the only recreational data are observational (Smyth & Lawlor) and cannot separate taper effect from runner characteristics.
- The effect of 4-week and longer tapers in marathoners is not reported in the abstract of Smyth & Lawlor; the 21-day cap is a conservative engine choice, not a tested limit.
- Interactions of taper with heat, altitude, travel and time-zone change are unresolved (Pyne 2009); no numeric adjustments possible.
- No evidence on age, sex or masters-specific taper parameters beyond a sex difference in association (Smyth & Lawlor); age effects on taper length are untested.
- Wearable-era data: no validated rule for using overnight HRV, resting HR or training-load metrics to individualize taper depth; Hug 2014 (n=9) used single-day post-exercise measures.
- Optimal weekly step split within the 41-60% band, and whether progressive beats step in short tapers, are not established; weekly multipliers are design choices.
- Pre-race week nutrition and sleep guidance rests on small physiological studies (n=8) without race-performance outcomes; sodium, hydration and caffeine strategies were not covered.
- No study of a shake-out run, a rest day or no change on the day before a race, or of the last day for quality work or a long run, was found (searched 2026-11-01); the cap on the day before and the count of easy days in rule K6 are grade D defaults (task RF3).
- A 2023-2026 consensus statement or recent marathon/ultra taper trial may exist but was not found in the searches run; recommend a targeted follow-up search for 2024-2026 work.
