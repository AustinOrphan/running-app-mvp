# Ultramarathon training characteristics

## Scope and quality of the evidence

Everything here is observational. There are no randomised trials of training for 50 km to 100 mile events in this corpus, and the nearest thing to a pooled estimate is a narrative review [knechtle-2018-ultra-physiology-review]. Most training data are self-reported by recreational runners. The engine should treat every number below as direction-of-effect support, not a dose-response curve. All 14 original DOIs resolved and matched; three papers were added ([hoffman-2014-ultra-health-injury], [stuempfle-2015-gi-distress-wser], [craddock-2020-acwr-two-oceans]).

## What is reasonably established

**Volume and experience drive ultra performance, more so as distance grows.** Mean weekly distance and mean training pace predict 100 km pace, with volume weighing more than pace at 100 km and the reverse for the marathon [tanda-2015-training-anthropometry-100km]. Finish time at 161 km falls with training volume [hoffman-2011-wser-vt100-factors], and DNF in a 56 km race is higher with fewer years of running, less race experience, lower weekly distance and less training per week [sewry-2022-safer-xxi-dnf]. Prior ultra finishes and marathon PB are the best single predictors [knechtle-2018-ultra-physiology-review]. Experienced active ultrarunners run a median of about 64 km/week [hoffman-2013-ultra-exercise-behavior], a descriptive figure only.

**Speed-based fitness predicts less as races get longer.** VO2max-test peak velocity predicts 50 and 80 km performance, but nothing in the tested set predicted 160 km results [coates-2021-physio-determinants-ultra]. At about 100 km, strength and body composition join aerobic capacity [pastor-2022-trail-determinants-distances]. Both studies are small and finisher-only.

**DNF and performance limiters at 100 miles are mostly practical.** Nausea or vomiting was the leading primary reason for dropping out (23.0% of non-finishers), and blisters and muscle pain were widely reported [hoffman-2011-wser-vt100-factors]; 35.6% of non-finishers cited GI symptoms [stuempfle-2015-gi-distress-wser]. Heat and age above 38 reduce finishing odds at Western States, and women over 38 fare worse than men [wegelin-2010-odds-finishing-161km]; heat (WBGT) also raises DNF at 56 km [sewry-2022-safer-xxi-dnf].

**Injury is the norm, and it hits the younger and less experienced.** In one year 64.6% of ultrarunners reported an injury with a median 14 lost days [hoffman-2014-ultra-health-injury]; 40% were injured around a 90 km race [burgess-2025-training-load-injury-gam].

## Contested

- **ACWR.** One 56 km cohort links ACWR above 1.5 to injury [craddock-2020-acwr-two-oceans]; a 90 km cohort finds minimal effect [burgess-2025-training-load-injury-gam]. Both agree that low absolute training is a risk marker, which may be reverse causation (injured runners train less).
- **First-timer risk.** First-time Western States starters finished more often, probably through selection [wegelin-2010-odds-finishing-161km]; the engine should not label first-timers high-risk on that basis alone.
- **Whether volume protects multi-day runners.** No pre-race variable separated finishers from non-finishers in a 44-runner transcontinental event [schutz-2019-tefr-prerace].

## Unknown (no support in this corpus)

Back-to-back long runs, the longest single run, time-on-feet targets, vertical-gain or downhill training dose, sleep-deprivation training, heat acclimation protocols, taper length for 100 miles, strength dose, post-race recovery duration, and any 200-mile single-stage training data. The engine must not present any of these as research-derived. Where a default is needed it is labelled an engine default (grade D).

## How the engine should use it

1. **Goal modelling.** Represent races with the Scheer descriptors (distance, gain/loss, surface, altitude, continuous or staged, support) [scheer-2020-defining-off-road-running]. Branch rules on them rather than on distance alone.
2. **Progression priority.** For goals of about 100 km and beyond, build weekly volume (within the athlete's own history and injury limits) before adding pace work; for 50 km and below, keep speed work [tanda-2015-training-anthropometry-100km] [coates-2021-physio-determinants-ultra].
3. **Readiness messages, not gates.** Compare the athlete's recent weekly distance, sessions per week and training pace with the goal, and show messages. Do not hard-code thresholds from a 56 km race; calibrate to the athlete's own history [sewry-2022-safer-xxi-dnf].
4. **Goal-time estimation.** Use marathon PB and prior 50-100 km results first. Show a range for 100 miles and beyond, and frame the goal as finishing first [knechtle-2018-ultra-physiology-review] [coates-2021-physio-determinants-ultra] [schutz-2019-tefr-prerace].
5. **Race-specific rehearsal.** Long runs should rehearse the race fuel plan (ISSN 30-50 g/h carbohydrate range as information), foot care and heat exposure, because GI distress, blisters and heat are the best-documented DNF and performance limiters [tiller-2019-issn-ultra-nutrition] [hoffman-2011-wser-vt100-factors] [stuempfle-2015-gi-distress-wser].
6. **Pacing.** Anchor race-day effort to heart rate at or below VT1 and flag planned effort above VT2 [fornasiero-2018-ultra-intensity]. Slow the plan when forecast WBGT is high.
7. **Injury logic.** Keep ACWR informational; use non-linear thinking, not a single threshold; be conservative with younger, less experienced and low-volume athletes [hoffman-2014-ultra-health-injury] [burgess-2025-training-load-injury-gam] [craddock-2020-acwr-two-oceans].
8. **Elite-case structure only.** Peak volume about 3 weeks before the race and a very short post-race recovery week appear in one elite athlete [byrne-2024-elite-24h-training]; never copy the volumes.

The app is not a medical or nutrition prescriber, and every flag should say so.

## Gaps

- No evidence in this corpus on back-to-back long runs, longest single run, time-on-feet targets, or weekly long-run share for 100-mile preparation; any such rule must be an explicitly labelled engine default.
- No randomised or intervention trials of ultra training plans; all training-outcome evidence is self-reported and observational.
- Scheer et al. 2021 ultra-trail position statements and Western States training-specific studies were not located in Europe PMC searches in this session; they should be searched under a separate query (Ultra Sports Science Foundation, Scheer, Millet).
- No data on 200-mile single-stage events, sleep-deprivation training, or multi-day energy-expenditure limits beyond the Thurber 2019 Science Advances paper (DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aaw0341, verified but not included).
- No evidence on vertical gain and downhill training dose, heat acclimation protocols, strength dose, or taper length for 100 miles.
- Sex-specific training and DNF evidence is thin (one age-by-sex interaction at Western States, women at higher DNF at 56 km); no female-specific volume or recovery data.
- Effect sizes and coefficients are not available from abstracts; full texts were not read, so numeric thresholds cannot be derived.
