# Ultramarathon training characteristics

## Scope and quality of the evidence

Everything here is observational. No randomised trial of training for 50 km to 100-mile events exists in this corpus. The nearest thing to a pooled estimate is a narrative review [knechtle-2018-ultra-physiology-review], and the only systematic reviews cover injury risk factors [viljoen-2022-trail-risk-factors] and sex differences [kelly-2023-sex-specific-ultra-review]. Most training data are self-reported by recreational runners, mostly men. Treat every number below as direction-of-effect support, not a dose-response curve. A second pass on the longest run, long-run share, time on feet and back-to-backs added 14 sources. Ten are peer-reviewed or conference studies (grade C). Three are practice sources: two Strava summaries and one interview with named coaches (grade D).

## What is reasonably established

**Weekly volume, frequency and experience drive ultra performance, more so as distance grows.** Mean weekly distance and training pace predict 100 km pace, with volume weighing more at 100 km [tanda-2015-training-anthropometry-100km]. In 166 male 100 km runners, weekly kilometres were the training variable linked to race time, while marathoners relied on speed [rust-2012-100km-vs-marathoners]. Finish time at 161 km falls as training volume rises [hoffman-2011-wser-vt100-factors]. Across 448 mountain-race finishers, weekly volume, weekly climbing and experience predicted performance, most clearly in longer races [belinchon-2021-mountain-race-predictors]. Training frequency correlated with time in a 217 km race (r = -0.59) [daniel-2024-217km-sleep-training]. In a 56 km cohort, DNF was higher with less weekly distance, fewer sessions and less experience [sewry-2022-safer-xxi-dnf]. These are consistent cross-sectional findings (grade C). They make weekly volume, not the single long run, the best-supported training lever.

**Speed-based fitness predicts less as races get longer.** Speed-based fitness predicts 50-80 km results but not 160 km [coates-2021-physio-determinants-ultra]. Strength and body composition matter at about 100 km [pastor-2022-trail-determinants-distances]. Pace-related variables predicted 62 km time in men and weekly volume did so in women, but the models were very small [oloughlin-2019-wellington-sex-predictors].

**DNF and performance limiters at 100 miles are mostly practical.** The documented limiters are GI distress, blisters, muscle pain, heat and age above 38 [hoffman-2011-wser-vt100-factors] [stuempfle-2015-gi-distress-wser] [wegelin-2010-odds-finishing-161km].

**Injury is common, and low volume, not high volume, is the recurring marker.** In ULTRA, 64.6% of runners reported an injury in the past year [hoffman-2014-ultra-health-injury]. In 697 trail runners, lower weekly volume, less climbing, fewer interval sessions, less sleep and less passive recovery went with injury [soppelsa-2025-trail-injury-performance]. Mac ultra entrants training for 46 km had a higher injury rate per 1000 h (3.09) than those training for 80 km (0.68) or 161 km (1.09) [viljoen-2024-mac-ultra-injury-rate]. Both studies may reflect reverse causation and differences in experience.

## The longest run, time on feet and back-to-backs

**No longest-run level is associated with finishing.** The evidence on this is all null or non-significant:

- In 26 runners at 161 km, finishers' longest run differed only moderately from non-finishers' (ES 0.73, P = 0.07), with no values reported [tan-2017-finishers-161km].
- At 66 km, "longest distance ever run" predicted finishing on its own but dropped out once blood pressure was adjusted for [taksaudom-2017-66km-predictors].
- In a Strava analysis of UTMB 2022, finishers and non-finishers were "nearly identical" on median longest run, though neither the sample nor the test was reported [outside-2023-strava-utmb].

The only positive signal is for finish time, not finishing. In a Leadville survey, a longer self-reported longest run (mean 51 miles, races included) went with a faster time [baldea-2009-leadville-training]. That source is a conference abstract with no effect sizes.

**What finishers do (practice, grade D).** Western States finishers had a median longest run of 50 miles, mostly races. Every finisher ran at least one 20-mile run, with about ten such runs on average, and 97% ran a 50K. In the median peak week of 81 miles the long run was 32 miles, about 40% of weekly distance, and the peak came 3-4 weeks out [outside-2023-strava-western-states]. Among UTMB runners, the median non-race long run in the last two months was only 14 miles, and the median number of weeks with a 20-mile-plus run was six. One elite runner reportedly capped long runs at 3-4 h because going past 5 h cost more than it gave [outside-2023-strava-utmb]. One named coach prescribes about one long run a month, progressing by time to a final 6-7 h run four weeks before a 100-miler. Another names nine hours a week for six weeks as a workable peak [outside-2015-orton-koop-100-miles].

**Injury and the long run.** No study links a given longest-run length or a back-to-back structure to injury [viljoen-2022-trail-risk-factors]. The one structural factor reported, double sessions on the same day, is not a back-to-back. Absence of evidence is not evidence of safety. The best evidence on session size is the general running cohort behind G-1, which found a higher hazard when a session exceeds 1.10 times the 30-day longest run [frandsen-2025-session-spike].

**So are the engine's numbers supported?** They are supported as defaults, not by outcomes:

- **Feasibility targets.** The 180/240/240/270 min targets sit at or below the floor of observed practice; 240 min is about a 20-24 mile trail run. Nothing ties them to finishing, so they remain grade D sketches.
- **The H2 cap.** The cap at the lesser of 6 h or 60% of expected race time falls between the named-coach conventions (3-5 h for one elite runner, 6-7 h for another coach). It sits below what finishers log once races are counted. No injury or performance evidence sets it, so it stays a conservative grade D default.
- **The ultra long-run share.** The 0.45 time share fits the roughly 40% distance share in the Western States peak week, because long runs are slower than weekday runs. It stays grade D.

## Contested

- **ACWR**: an injury association above 1.5 in one study [craddock-2020-acwr-two-oceans], a minimal effect in another [burgess-2025-training-load-injury-gam].
- **First-timer risk** at Western States is probably a selection effect [wegelin-2010-odds-finishing-161km].
- **Whether volume protects multi-day runners** [schutz-2019-tefr-prerace].
- **How long the longest run should be.** Practice sources range from 3-4 h to over 9 h (races included), and the coaching opinions contradict each other.
- **How fast to progress.** The coaching convention of +1 h per month (2 h to 3 h) sits in G-1's warn band. G-1 is grade B and wins.

## Unknown

Unknown are any dose-response for the longest run, back-to-backs, time on feet or long-run share, and any outcome trial of a long-run cap. Also unknown: sex-specific long-run guidance, which a systematic review found too weakly evidenced to formulate [kelly-2023-sex-specific-ultra-review], vertical and downhill dose, taper length for 100 miles, and 200-mile training data.

## How the engine should use it

1. **Volume first.** For goals of 100 km and more, grow weekly time before pace work or the long run. The volume-first rule moves from three sources to seven consistent ones (still grade C).
2. **Feasibility.** For ultras, D4 measures both the longest run and weekly running time. The long-run target Lt is the least of d4LongRun for the class, the H2 cap and the long-run share times the larger of the class peak week (peakWeeklyMin) and the start week, so feasibility never counts weeks toward a run the planner would not schedule. The weekly path grows from the start week (the larger of the typical week and the start-path floor week) toward peakWeeklyMin with the weekly ramp, and the label uses the slower of the two paths. Labels only, never a limit. With defaults the plan's peak long run for 50 km (390 x 0.45 = 175 min) and 100 km (480 x 0.45 = 216 min) falls short of the d4LongRun targets of 180 and 240 min; the target follows the plan.
3. **Keep the numbers.** Keep d4LongRun 180/240/240/270 min, the H2 cap (6 h, 0.60), the H3 limits, the 0.45 share and peakWeeklyMin.ultra100k at 480 min (540 min would add load on grade D). Add the new citations and label each as a default.
4. **Race simulation (design intent only).** One longer rehearsal of up to 8 h, or an entered tune-up race standing in for it, no later than 3 weeks before race day and never in the taper, is a design intent. No code path reads the 8 h value, so neither the rules nor the app describe the simulation as available. After a tune-up of 100 km or more, H12 applies.
5. **Messaging.** Never present a longest run as required to finish. Present it as a sketch that weekly training supports.
6. **No sex-specific values.** Note that ultra training data come mostly from men.
7. **Practical limiters.** Long runs keep rehearsing fuel, foot care and heat [tiller-2019-issn-ultra-nutrition].

The app is not a medical or nutrition prescriber.

## Gaps

- No study gives a dose-response for longest single run, time on feet, long-run share or back-to-back structure against finishing, performance or injury at 50 km, 100 km, 100 miles or beyond; the strongest finisher vs non-finisher data are n = 26 (P = 0.07) and an unreported-N Strava comparison.
- No outcome trial or cohort tests a long-run cap (6 h, 60% of race time, or any other); the cap remains a default.
- Back-to-back long runs are absent from every peer-reviewed source found; only coaching convention and Western States training-camp descriptions mention them.
- Longest-run figures in practice sources include races and supported events, so non-race training long runs are poorly described (UTMB median non-race long run of 14 miles is the only figure).
- No data for single-stage races beyond 100 miles (200-mile events); 270 min and the 6 h cap there are pure extrapolation.
- Ultra training data are mostly male; no female-specific long-run or volume data (one 62 km study with df = 6).
- No evidence on how a tune-up race in the build affects injury, recovery time or race outcome, or on its best timing.
- Full texts were not read; effect sizes and thresholds behind Tan 2017, Baldea 2009 and the Strava comparisons are unavailable.
- Scheer/Ultra Sports Science Foundation training position statements and published Western States or UTMB training cohorts with DNF outcomes remain unsearched or unavailable.
