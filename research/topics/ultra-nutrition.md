# Ultramarathon nutrition, hydration and gut training

## What is established

**Daily diet.** The ISSN position stand [tiller-2019-issn-ultra-nutrition] gives the training baseline: carbohydrate about 60% of energy (5-8 g/kg/day) to offset chronic glycogen depletion, and protein about 1.6 g/kg/day, up to 2.5 g/kg/day in demanding blocks. Podlogar and Wallis [podlogar-2022-cho-new-horizons] add that "train-low" has limited utility for performance and that carbohydrate should be periodised to the demand of each session.

**Gut problems are the norm.** In the 161-km Western States, 96% of 272 respondents reported at least one GI symptom, nausea was most common among those whose performance suffered or who dropped out, and it peaked in the hottest segment [stuempfle-2015-gi-distress-wser]. The mechanism is well characterised: GI injury, permeability and malabsorption rise with intensity, duration and heat, with a threshold around 2 h at about 60% VO2max [costa-2017-eigs-sr].

**Overdrinking, not sodium loss, drives most exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH).** The 2015 consensus [hewbutler-2015-eah-consensus], as relayed by the 2017 update [hewbutler-2017-eah-update], names overconsumption of hypotonic fluid plus non-osmotic vasopressin release as the primary cause. Sodium can attenuate the fall in blood sodium but cannot prevent EAH when fluid intake is excessive. Drinking to thirst is the most individualised strategy, and fixed intake ranges are explicitly not appropriate. A prospective 161-km cohort found that supplement sodium intake did not differ between hyponatremic and normonatremic finishers and that overhydration was the primary characteristic [hoffman-2015-sodium-supplementation-eah]. Weight loss up to about 3% was not harmful to performance [hoffman-2013-eah-hydration-161km].

## What is contested

- **Race carbohydrate dose.** The ISSN stand suggests 30-50 g/h in racing [tiller-2019-issn-ultra-nutrition], reflecting real-world tolerance. Jeukendrup's physiology-based guideline proposes about 90 g/h for ultra-endurance using multiple transportable carbohydrates [jeukendrup-2014-cho-intake]. Two small studies favour 120 g/h on recovery and critical-power surrogates [urdampilleta-2020-cho-120, norte-2026-graded-cho-cp], but neither measured ultra racing, and a higher-CHO strategy in highly trained athletes gave no extra performance benefit and more upper-GI symptoms in walkers [king-2022-high-cho-gut-training]. A model suggests women may need more exogenous CHO at marathon intensity [lukasiewicz-2024-cho-sex-modeling], but this is simulation at elite pace, not ultra pace.
- **Gut training.** Two small RCTs showed fewer GI symptoms and less malabsorption after 2 weeks of repeated carbohydrate feeding, with possible performance gains [costa-2017-gut-training, miall-2018-gut-challenge]. The systematic review found only eight small studies, mixed outcomes, and no change in injury or permeability markers [martinez-2023-gut-training-sr].
- **Sodium and the fluid target.** The ISSN stand recommends sodium above 575 mg/L and 450-750 mL/h [tiller-2019-issn-ultra-nutrition], while the EAH literature says sodium does not prevent EAH and fluid should follow thirst. The older ACSM stand's 2% body-weight-loss limit [acsm-2007-fluid-replacement] conflicts with thirst-based guidance in ultras.

## What is unknown

There are no ultra-distance RCTs of gut training or of 90-120 g/h. The dose-response in low-intensity 100-mile racing, sex differences, optimal gut-training frequency and duration, and the ceiling of safe fluid intake are untested. Caffeine, low-FODMAP and ketone strategies are mentioned in the ISSN stand but were not verified here beyond its abstract.

## How the engine should use this

1. Use a three-tier fuelling model: floor (30-50 g/h, 150-400 kcal/h), standard (60 g/h, gut-trained), advanced opt-in (up to 90 g/h, then 90-120 g/h), each gated on the previous tier being tolerated in training. Label tiers above the floor as low-certainty.
2. Gut training is an optional 2-4 week block of repeated long or steady runs at the intended race rate with a 0-4 symptom log; progress only if symptoms do not worsen. Never claim guaranteed performance or gut-barrier protection.
3. Present fluid as a range, with "drink to thirst" and a no-forcing rule. Never output a fixed intake above thirst. Treat sodium as taste/cramp support, not EAH prophylaxis. Add a safety card (weight gain, nausea, headache, confusion, swelling mean seek medical care) and a hot-race / long-race (> 17 h) alert.
4. In heat, lower intensity and expected GI tolerance, and add a nausea contingency (simpler fuel, slower pace). Add a medical disclaimer for athletes with GI disease.
5. Do not apply sex multipliers, body-mass scaling of in-race CHO, or train-low prescriptions as performance tools.
6. Where a number has no direct evidence (progression step size, minimum weeks, session frequency), the engine uses a conservative default graded D and shows it as a default.

None of this is medical advice; the engine presents ranges and defaults, not prescriptions.

## Gaps

- No ultra-distance (50 km to 100+ mile) randomised trials of gut training or of 90-120 g/h carbohydrate; extrapolation from 2-4 h running, cycling and marathon-scale trials.
- Session count, frequency and progression step size for gut training are not established; Costa 2017 abstract gives no frequency and the one protocol with detail (Miall) used daily 1 h sessions in 18 runners.
- Sex and age effects on carbohydrate tolerance, gut training response and EAH risk are mostly unstudied outside a marathon-pace simulation; the Hew-Butler 2017 review mentions possible higher EAH risk in menstruating females, only as a hypothesis.
- The Hew-Butler 2015 consensus text could not be accessed (no abstract; publisher 403); its findings are verified only secondarily through the 2017 update.
- Sodium: unresolved conflict between the ISSN > 575 mg/L suggestion and evidence that supplementation does not prevent EAH; no trial of sodium dose in ultras.
- Caffeine, low-FODMAP, ketone and protein-in-race recommendations in the ISSN stand were not independently verified beyond its abstract; protein 5-10 g/h in racing has no outcome evidence in the corpus.
- Heat-specific CHO and fluid adjustments lack numeric thresholds; Jeukendrup 2014 tier details (e.g. 1-2 h and glucose:fructose ratio) need full-text verification before use.
- Tiller 2019 carbohydrate range (30-50 g/h) versus Jeukendrup/Urdampilleta (60-120 g/h) is not resolved by any direct ultra comparison; Norte 2026 and Urdampilleta 2020 authors' potential commercial interests were not checked.
