# Age and masters runners

## What is established

**Decline exists, is accelerating, and is mostly a VO2max story.** Endurance performance is roughly maintained to about age 35, declines modestly through 50-60, and falls progressively faster afterwards [tanaka-2008-masters-endurance]. The same shape shows up in world-record data (near-linear to the 70s, then accelerating) [lazarus-2017-master-athletes-trajectory], in 83,209 longitudinal and cross-sectional Swedish masters results (post-70 decline 1.7x steeper in men, 1.4x in women) [ganse-2020-longitudinal-masters], and in directly measured VO2peak (about 3% per decade in the 20s rising to about 20% in the 70s) [letnes-2020-vo2peak-hunt]. Mechanistically, VO2max (stroke volume, HRmax, a-vO2 difference) falls, threshold follows, and running economy does not change in trained adults [tanaka-2008-masters-endurance], [romberger-2024-master-marathon-case]. Even runners who keep training hard lose VO2max at roughly 8-15% per decade between 50 and 70, and those who largely stop lose much more [pollock-1997-older-track-athletes].

**HRmax falls with age, independent of training and sex at the group level.** 208 - 0.7 x age [tanaka-2001-hrmax-equation] or 211 - 0.64 x age [nes-2013-hunt-hrmax]; both beat 220 - age for older adults, but individual scatter is about +/-10 bpm. Training does not stop the decline (about 5-7 bpm per decade in older athletes) [pollock-1997-older-track-athletes], [hawkins-2003-vo2max-decline].

## What is contested

**The size of the decline.** Hawkins' 10% per decade [hawkins-2003-vo2max-decline] is an older, mixed-design figure; HUNT shows lower decline before 50 and higher after 70 [letnes-2020-vo2peak-hunt]; sub-3 marathoners kept speed loss near 0.67% per year, under 7% per decade, to about 60 [lepers-2021-sub3-marathon-decades]. Elite and survivor-selected cohorts understate what recreational runners will see, and slower runners appear to slow faster [stones-2019-age-differences-marathon]. Sex: HUNT found a larger VO2peak decline in men, Ganse found no sex difference in annual percentage performance decline in most disciplines; female-specific VO2max loss was suggested to be harder to reduce by training [hawkins-2003-vo2max-decline]. The engine should be sex-neutral by default.

**Whether masters recover slower.** Reviews are inconclusive, with confounding by inactivity and unrepresentative damage protocols [fell-2008-aging-muscle-recovery], [borges-2016-masters-recovery-review]. The one controlled interval study (cyclists, n=17) found equal physical recovery over 48 h but poorer perceived recovery in masters at 48 h (moderate effect sizes with wide intervals) [borges-2018-masters-hiit-recovery]. Note: the Borges 2018 paper is that experiment; the Borges review is 2016.

**Injury.** Masters runners reported more injuries and more calf, Achilles and hamstring problems, but they also trained more (miles, days per week) and run frequency itself raised risk in both age groups [mckean-2006-masters-injury]. Age and exposure cannot be separated.

## What is unknown

There is no running-specific study of recovery time in masters, no trial of cycle length (3:1 vs 2:1 vs longer) or session spacing by age, no validation of WMA age-grading tables in the corpus (Stones critiques record-list curves but does not mention WMA), little on training adaptation to a given programme in older runners (the evidence is observational), and almost nothing on ultra-distance masters beyond race-level descriptive trends [knechtle-2012-100km-age], [hoffman-2009-western-states-trends].

## How the engine should use this

1. **Age is a modifier of projection, not of prescription.** Decline is partly caused by being unable to train as hard or as much [tanaka-2008-masters-endurance]; cutting volume or quality by age alone would cause the decline it assumes. Adapt load from observed tolerance, soreness, performance and injury data.
2. **Projection curve.** Flat to 35, then a per-year speed decline defaulting to about 0.7% per year (band 0.5-1.0), multiplied by 1.4-1.7 after 70, and conditioned on consistency: the optimistic Lepers rate only for runners who train consistently, a larger rate (up to about 1% per year) otherwise [pollock-1997-older-track-athletes]. Expose these as parameters and refit to the runner's own pace-at-HR and race history; the data are a prior, not a law.
3. **Recovery.** Keep standard hard-session spacing (about 48 h). Collect a post-session perceived-recovery rating; for masters, if soreness, fatigue or motivation are still poor at 48 h, defer or convert the next quality session. Do not hard-code an age multiplier; allow a small data-driven recovery factor.
4. **Intensity and strength.** Keep a weekly quality session where tolerated because intensity appears to matter more than minutes for maintaining VO2peak at higher ages [letnes-2020-vo2peak-hunt]; include resistance training and calf/posterior-chain work for 40+ [hawkins-2003-vo2max-decline], [mckean-2006-masters-injury].
5. **HR zones.** Seed HRmax from Tanaka, show Nes as an alternative, and replace with a rolling observed maximum from reliable hard efforts; allow HRmax to fall over years.
6. **Age grading.** Show only as a labelled comparison to elite standards; do not set targets from it [stones-2019-age-differences-marathon].
7. **Ultra goals.** No age decline before about 50 for 100 km to 100-mile goals, consistent with best times at 30-49 (men) / 30-54 (women) and with Western States fastest men at 30-49 [knechtle-2012-100km-age], [hoffman-2009-western-states-trends]; low confidence.

The evidence base here is thin (one narrative-review majority, two elite-biased cohorts), so most numbers carry grade C-D and should be exposed as tunable defaults with provenance.

## Gaps

- No running-specific study of post-session recovery time in masters; the only controlled recovery data are 17 cyclists and show a perceptual (not physical) delay.
- No evidence on cycle length (e.g. 3:1 vs 2:1 vs 4:1 build/recovery weeks) or session spacing by age beyond the 48 h recovery study; defaults are engineering conventions.
- No peer-reviewed validation of WMA age-grading factor tables found; age grading is treated as a display-only comparison.
- Little training-adaptation evidence for older runners: Pollock 1997 (n=21) and observational HUNT data only; no randomised trials of periodisation or intensity distribution by age.
- Sex-specific and menopause-related effects on decline and recovery are unresolved (Letnes: larger decline in men; Hawkins: women may not reduce loss; Ganse: no sex difference); no data for ultra-distance women.
- Injury evidence is a single retrospective relay-event survey with age-exposure confounding; no prospective cohort of masters runners with load data.
- Age-by-heat, altitude, and wearable optical HR accuracy in older runners were outside the retrieved evidence.
- Decline data for ultra-distance (100 mile+) runners over 60 are essentially absent beyond race-level trend descriptions.
- Bone density, tendon and menopause-related considerations for load progression were not covered.
- Wake caveat on rule age-strength-training: the evidence id listed 'pollock-1997-older-track-announced-trend-placeholder' should be read as pollock-1997-older-track-athletes (weight-training trend only).
