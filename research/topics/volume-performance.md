# Training volume and race performance

## Summary for the plan engine

All 12 researcher entries resolved in Crossref and Europe PMC, and their numbers match the abstracts. One author order was wrong (Knechtle and Tanda 2015, now `knechtle-2015-marathon-100km-training`). Two were downgraded (Doherty A to B, Toresdahl B to C). One implication was rewritten because the abstract does not support it (the 1.3 ACWR result). Two papers were added: Soligard 2016 and Knechtle 2010. Only abstracts were read, so every number below is an abstract number.

### What is well established

**Volume tracks marathon performance almost everywhere it has been measured.** Doherty found every volume-type covariate (weekly distance, runs per week, peak week, longest run, count of 32 km+ runs, hours, pace) associated with faster finish times across 137 cohorts [doherty-2020-marathon-training-meta]. Muniz-Pumares reproduced this in 119,452 runners: the mean was 45.1 km/week (SD 26.4) and the fastest group ran more than three times the volume of slower groups [muniz-pumares-2025-marathon-tid]. Fokkema, Yamaguchi, Casado and Gordon agree in direction in smaller samples [fokkema-2020-half-marathon-volume-long-run, yamaguchi-2023-volume-frequency-run-distance, casado-2021-easy-run-volume, gordon-2017-recreational-marathon-characteristics]. The engine should therefore scale its targets to the goal-time band, not use one template.

**Consistency has a measurable cost when broken.** A full-rest gap of 7 or more days in the 16 weeks before a marathon cost 5-8% of finish time against the same runner's undisrupted marathon; gaps under 6 days were common and tolerable [feely-2022-training-disruptions]. This is the best within-runner design in the set.

**Single equations are not trustworthy.** Riegel from a half-marathon gives marathon times at least 10 min too fast for half of runners [vickers-2016-race-times]; no one published equation is best, and sex, course and weather are often missing [keogh-2019-marathon-prediction-equations].

### What is contested or weakly supported

- **Diminishing returns.** None of the papers estimates a diminishing-returns curve. The associations are monotone and ecological: faster cohorts differ in talent, history and age, so "more volume goes with faster times" cannot be read as "more volume would make this runner faster". The engine must not extrapolate the curve upward for one athlete.
- **Floors.** Fokkema's thresholds (half: >32 km/wk and longest run >21 km; marathon: <40 km/wk slower, >65 km/wk faster, longest run <25 km slower) come from categorised self-reports. The lower confidence limit for the 40 km finding is close to zero [fokkema-2020-half-marathon-volume-long-run]. They are usable as soft floors, not as proven minimums.
- **Frequency vs volume vs long run.** Yamaguchi found that at a fixed monthly volume frequency and long-run length added nothing, but this is men only and cross-sectional [yamaguchi-2023-volume-frequency-run-distance]. Doherty and Fokkema find long runs matter. The engine should treat volume as primary and long run as a threshold feature (at least about 21-25 km), not something to maximise.
- **Intensity.** Fast runners are mostly pyramidal and add volume as easy running, with Z2/Z3 time roughly constant [muniz-pumares-2025-marathon-tid, casado-2021-easy-run-volume]. This is descriptive, not a trial of distributions, and zones are derived from the same data.
- **Ultras.** Volume mattered more than pace for 100 km in one dataset [tanda-2015-training-anthropometry-100km], while training speed had the larger correlation in another [knechtle-2010-100km-predictors], and volume did not predict 24 h results in n=15 [knechtle-2009-24h-run-training-volume]. No source supports a 100-mile volume rule.
- **Injury and spikes.** One cohort found 40% injured during training and a small per-day OR (1.06) for days with a 7:28-day ratio of 1.5 or more [toresdahl-2023-nyc-marathon-training-injury]; Fokkema found no volume-injury link. The IOC statement is qualitative [soligard-2016-ioc-load]. A ratio guard is reasonable but weakly evidenced.

### What is unknown

Minimum training to finish a marathon (no included paper estimates it; Doherty reports a 7.27% non-finish rate with no training breakdown); how age, sex and temperature modify volume response; the shape of any individual dose-response; volume for 100-mile races; whether peak volume or total consistency matters more once both are controlled.

### How the engine should use this

1. Derive weekly volume, long-run and session-length targets from the goal time and the athlete's current base, and show them as floors for competitive goals and sketches otherwise (project convention: no "behind" copy).
2. Build volume by adding easy running; keep quality sessions a modest fixed share.
3. Apply a 7-day-gap flag and graded-restart logic; plan the last 4-6 weeks to avoid breaks.
4. Apply the ratio guard softly, with athlete feedback, and never treat it as proof of safety.
5. Output a prediction interval from at least two sources and include mileage, sex, age and course; for ultras widen the interval and weight recent race speed.
6. Label D-grade defaults as defaults in the UI and in code.

All of this is population-level association evidence and must not be presented to the user as a guarantee.

## Gaps

- Minimum training to finish a marathon: no included paper estimates it. Doherty gives only a 7.27% non-finish rate; a search for finisher-focused training studies found none in the abstracts examined.
- No diminishing-returns estimate: all volume-performance evidence is monotone and between-cohort. Individual dose-response, and where gains flatten or injury risk rises, are unmeasured.
- Peak volume vs consistency: Feely gives a gap cost but no paper compares a high peak against steady moderate volume.
- Numeric coefficients are in full texts that were not read (Doherty regression slopes, Emig optimal-training values, Fokkema units, Tanda/Knechtle equations).
- Women, older runners and slower finishers are under-represented or analysed only by group (Yamaguchi men only, Doherty 25% female, Knechtle cohorts mostly male); age and sex modifiers of volume response are not quantified here.
- Temperature and heat effects on training load and performance are not covered by this topic's entries.
- 100-mile and multi-day races: no training-volume evidence in this set beyond 100 km and 24 h correlational studies.
- Injury evidence is thin: ACWR on distance only, one cohort, small effect, and the 1.3 threshold result is not in the abstract.
- Long-run number and distance: the effect of 32 km+ runs is directional only; no study here tests an optimal count or length.
