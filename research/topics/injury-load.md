# Running injury risk and load progression

## What is established

**Injuries are common, previous injury is the strongest marker, and novices carry the most risk per hour.** Pooled incidence is 17.8 injuries per 1000 h in novices and 7.7 in recreational runners [videbaek-2015-incidence]. Previous injury predicts new injury across reviews [hulme-2017-risk-protective] [vanderworp-2015-risk-factors]; a later review ranks a previous running injury as the strongest factor for long-distance runners, and no running experience and low volume as strong factors for short-distance runners [vanpoppel-2021-overuse-risk-factors-review]. In a Couch-to-5k cohort a previous injury gave OR 7.56 (2.06-27.75) for a new one [relph-2023-couch-to-5k-injury]. For bone stress injury (BSI), previous BSI (OR 4.99) and female sex (OR 2.31) are the supported factors [wright-2015-stress-fracture-meta]; overall injury rates do not differ by sex (RR 0.99) [hollander-2021-sex-differences]. Published risk-prediction models are low quality [vanpoppel-2021-overuse-risk-factors-review], so the engine should not compute a personal injury probability.

**No progression threshold has trial support.** The only randomised trial of a progression rule found no difference between a 10%-rule graded programme (20.8% injured) and a standard programme (20.3%) in novices [buist-2008-graded-training]. A systematic review of 36 prospective studies (23,047 runners) found the evidence on weekly distance, duration, frequency, intensity and changes in them conflicting, and advised caution about recommending any progression [fredette-2022-training-parameters-injury-review]; earlier reviews agree [nielsen-2012-training-errors] [damsted-2018-training-load-change]. No other trial was found. Load-injury studies are observational and prone to bias; target trial emulation on session-level data is the proposed route to causal estimates [wang-2024-target-trial-load]. Every threshold below is an association, not a proven effect of capping.

**A single-session spike is still the best running-specific signal.** In 5205 Garmin-tracked runners, a session more than 10% longer than the longest run of the prior 30 days carried HRR 1.64 (10-30%), 1.52 (30-100%) and 2.28 (>100%) against up to a 10% increase; the week-to-week ratio showed no association and the 1:3-week ACWR an inverse one [frandsen-2025-session-spike]. Most overuse injuries looked sudden (6.9% had a reported problem 7 days before, 11.1% at 28 days) [frandsen-2025-sudden-onset]. No new source tests another session threshold, so 1.10x stays.

## Weekly ramp: what the new studies add

- Damsted 2019 (261 runners, half-marathon build): at day 21, a weekly increase of 20-60% was associated with more injury than under 20% (risk difference 22.6%, 0.9-44.3%), not at days 56 or 98, with no modification by schedule [damsted-2019-weekly-distance-change]. This supports a ceiling somewhere around 20-30%, not a 10% line.
- Nielsen 2013 (60 novices): the week before injury showed an 86% progression, while uninjured novices averaged 22.1% a week; the authors put the deleterious zone above about 30% [nielsen-2013-gps-progression]. Nielsen 2014 found no overall difference across <10%, 10-30% and >30% groups, with a non-significant signal for distance-related injuries above 30% (HR 1.59, 0.96-2.66) [nielsen-2014-weekly-progression].
- Youth cross-country runners showed no association between week-to-week change and next-week injury (p >= 0.54) [joachim-2024-week-to-week-youth]; not transferable to adults.
- 92 published sub-elite marathon plans build at 5-9% a week on average [knopp-2024-marathon-plans]. That is practice (grade D), with no outcome data.

So the 10% soft ramp matches coaching practice but has no evidence as a threshold (grade D), and the 30% over 2 weeks hard ceiling sits where the weak cohort signals are (grade C). Neither number should move: tightening the ceiling to 20% a week would rest on one time point of one small cohort, and loosening the novice ramp toward 20-25% would rest on 60 novices.

## Reference window and exposure

No study compares a 30-day reference with any other window. The calculation method changes which sessions are flagged: in 22,839 sessions the same cutoffs (weekly +30% or ACWR 1.5) flagged 33.4% with a weekly method and 16.2-25.8% with rolling or EWMA methods [cloosterman-2024-acwr-methods]. The engine already compares a week with the highest of the 2 preceding weeks and skips recovery and taper weeks; that handles the main artefact. The 30-day window cannot tell a first-time build from a return after a break, and injury timing may lag the causal run; no study addresses either point. A return after a break gives a lower reference, which errs conservative, so keep the window. A completed race of marathon distance or longer (42.195 km or more) can raise the reference above anything trained, so it does not count toward the reference, in the check and in the generator alike; shorter races count as runs (grade D).

Duration as an exposure now has weak support: in 461 GPS-tracked runners, a duration-based ACWR was associated with injury after adjustment (HR 1.33, 1.02-1.74), but not once runners injured at baseline were excluded [cloosterman-2026-gps-load-injury]. It is indirect support only: the cohort measured an ACWR-type ratio, not a session against the longest run, so "G-1 on duration" stays a grade D convention. Ascent and descent remain untested: the trail-running risk review lists no load or elevation factor [viljoen-2022-trail-risk-factors], and exposure should be chosen by plausible mechanism [impellizzeri-2023-exposure-dose], so gain and descent guards stay grade D. Volume progression and pace progression showed no significant interaction (relative >10%: RD 8.1%, -9.3 to 25.6) [ramskov-2022-volume-pace-interaction]. That gives no basis for a combined pace-and-volume threshold. The existing one-lever-at-a-time rule stays because it is the more conservative rule.

## Novice versus experienced runners

Nothing supports looser limits for experienced runners. Runners with fewer than 1 year or more than 40 years of running had the most injuries, and those running under 2 times or under 25 km a week had more [abrahamson-2025-self-reported-characteristics]. In trail runners, more experience went with more injury [viljoen-2022-trail-risk-factors]. Pooled incidence was higher in competitive (62.6%) than novice runners (14.9%), probably reflecting exposure and follow-up [fredette-2022-training-parameters-injury-review]. The same G-1 and G-2 apply to everyone. New, low-frequency and recently injured runners get the conservative profile.

Couch-to-5k style programmes lack an evidence base: in a modified 9-week programme only 27.3% completed, and dropouts linked non-completion to injury and the fixed progressive design [relph-2023-couch-to-5k-injury]. A starter plan is a default, should let the runner repeat a week without penalty, and should size its first sessions from the runner's own recent runs through G-1 rather than one fixed length.

## Tissue capacity after a reduction in load, and the load-change framework

Added 2026-10-25 from six papers: two small tendon studies, one tendon meta-analysis, two bone stress injury reviews and one conceptual framework. All are context for a caution; none gives a value for the engine.

**Tendon stiffness fell within weeks of complete unloading, and a gain built over months was lost within a month.** In 9 young men whose leg was suspended for 23 days (8 men served as controls), patellar tendon stiffness fell 9.8% by day 14 and 29.3% by day 23, and knee-extensor torque 14.8% and 21.0% [deboer-2007-ulls-tendon-time-course]. In 9 young men who trained the plantar flexors isometrically for 3 months, Achilles tendon stiffness did not change until month 2, was 50.3% higher at month 3 and was back at its pre-training level after 1 month of detraining [kubo-2012-achilles-training-detraining]. For comparison, VO2max fell 7% by day 21 after endurance athletes stopped training [coyle-1984-detraining-time-course]. The people, methods and kinds of unloading differ, so the three together suggest that tissue capacity and aerobic fitness need not fall at the same pace and show nothing more. Both tendon studies are small, in young non-runners, with complete unloading or cessation of one training type; neither tests a partial reduction in running, a retraining phase or an injury outcome.

**Tendon capacity is built by loading magnitude over weeks.** In a meta-analysis of 27 studies (37 interventions of 8 weeks or more, 264 healthy adults aged 18-50), tendon stiffness rose (standardised mean difference 0.70, 95% CI 0.51-0.88) and depended significantly on loading intensity, not on contraction type; the effect was larger, not significantly so, at 12 weeks or more [bohm-2015-tendon-adaptation-meta]. Every pooled intervention lasted 8 weeks or more, so the review cannot say that a shorter build fails, and it holds no unloading arm.

**Bone damage and repair run over weeks to months, and the reviews give a framework, not a rate.** A bone stress injury results from microdamage forming faster than it is removed, and its risk relates to the load on the bone and to the bone's ability to resist it [warden-2014-bsi-long-distance-runners] (clinical commentary, level 5; abstract read). A narrative review states that all BSIs in runners arise from an error in workload, that there is a range of optimal workload and not one value, that cortical resorption takes about 4 weeks and replacement 3 months to a year, that BSIs began about 3-4 weeks after a large change in workload in military recruits, that tolerance of load changes varies much between individuals and skeletal sites, and that a single progression rule is unlikely to apply to all runners; it advises raising duration before intensity [warden-2021-bsi-optimal-workload]. Those statements are the review's summaries of other sources (grade D) and carry no safe weekly increase.

**Capacity against load, per structure, is the frame for any credit given to past training.** A conceptual framework splits running injury into the capacity of a structure entering a session, the cumulative load of the session, the reduction in capacity during it and exceeding it [bertelsen-2017-rri-etiology-framework]. Weekly minutes measure load. None of the six papers measures the capacity of a structure in runners.

**What these papers support.** Caution after a large reduction in running: tendon capacity can fall within 2 to 4 weeks of complete unloading in young non-runners, is built by loading over weeks, and bone damage and its repair take weeks to months, so a workload error can show weeks after it. That is consistent with the existing limits (G-1 as the session guard, the hard 2-week ceiling of G-2) and gives no support to crediting a past block to the length of a session.

**What they do not support.** No paper gives a retraining rate for runners returning to a prior peak. None dates tendon or bone capacity in runners who keep running a little, tests a ceiling taken from a runner's own history, or reports the injury rate of a rebuild after two to eight weeks of reduced running. They cannot raise the grade of any rule; a faster rebuild after a reduction stays a grade D default.

## What is contested

**ACWR.** Team-sport reviews report an association [griffin-2020-acwr-systematic-review] [gabbett-2016-paradox]; critiques cite artefacts and no causal evidence [impellizzeri-2020-acwr-pitfalls] [wang-2020-acwr-lessons]. The largest running cohort found an inverse association [frandsen-2025-session-spike], and the second GPS cohort's small association disappeared when baseline-injured runners were excluded [cloosterman-2026-gps-load-injury]. ACWR stays display-only. **Sex:** use the meta-analysis [hollander-2021-sex-differences] over the older review [vanderworp-2015-risk-factors].

## What is unknown

No trial tests a session-spike or ramp rule. Nothing compares reference windows. Elevation, intensity and heat spikes are untested. Ultra and multi-day build-ups have no load-injury evidence [hulme-2017-risk-protective] [viljoen-2022-trail-risk-factors]. Return-to-run ramps after injury, deload frequency and age effects remain defaults. Tendon and bone capacity in runners after a partial reduction in running, and how fast recreational runners regain an earlier weekly level, are not in the corpus (the tissue papers above study complete unloading in young non-runners).

## How the engine should use this

1. G-1 stays the primary guard: 1.10x warn, 2.00x override, 30-day window of completed runs, on distance (grade B) and on duration, gain and descent (grade D). A completed race of marathon distance or longer does not count toward the 30-day reference; shorter races count.
2. G-2 stays at 10% soft (relabel grade D, a default) and 30% over 2 weeks hard (grade C), compared with the highest of the 2 preceding loading weeks.
3. No loosening by experience. Treat a logged average under 2 runs a week as conservative (novice ramp and 2:1 recovery).
4. Starter plans size sessions with G-1 (the start paths of the beginners-return topic) and let the runner repeat a week.
5. Copy says "associated with", never "causes" or "prevents", and never computes a personal injury risk.
6. The tissue papers are a reason for caution after a reduction in running, not a source of values. A faster rebuild toward a level the runner held earlier is a grade D default and leaves G-1 and the 2-week ceiling in force.

## Caveats

All new entries were verified from abstracts only (Knopp also from its full text). The Frandsen results are hazard rates relative to a reference band, not causal limits. The six tissue and framework entries of 2026-10-25 were also verified from abstracts (Warden 2021 also from its open-access author manuscript); the timing statements of the bone reviews are theirs, taken from other sources.

## Gaps

- No randomised trial of a session-spike rule or of any weekly ramp other than Buist 2008 (10% rule in novices, null); no target trial emulation of progression rules in runners exists in this set.
- No study compares reference windows (7, 14, 30, 60 days) for the session guard; Cloosterman 2024 shows the method changes flags but has no injury outcome.
- Duration evidence is ACWR-based (Cloosterman 2026), not a session-versus-longest comparison; the ACWR window lengths are not in the abstract.
- Elevation (gain, descent), intensity and heat spikes are untested as exposures; the trail-running risk review lists no load or elevation factor, and later updates of that living review were not checked.
- No evidence on progression or long-run caps for ultra and multi-day events (including 200+ mile races); H2's 6 h / 60% cap and any longer cap remain conventions.
- No evidence on 5K peak long-run length; Knopp 2024 covers marathon plans only (longest runs 31-35 km).
- Damsted 2019 compares under 20% vs 20-60% only, so it cannot locate a threshold; a per-week 20% ceiling is untested.
- Abrahamson 2025's structured-programme estimate has an internally inconsistent sign in the abstract; the full text was not read.
- All new sources verified at abstract level except Knopp 2024 (full text); Frandsen 2025 full text still unread.
- Return-to-run ramps after injury, deload frequency and age effects on limits remain grade D defaults.
- Whether logging planned versus actual sessions in the app could support a later target trial emulation is a design question not addressed by any study.
- No running study of the injury rate when a runner returns to an earlier weekly level or long run after two to eight weeks of reduced running (G-1's cohort contains such runs and does not separate them).
- No time course of tendon or bone capacity in runners after a partial reduction in running; the tendon time courses are 23 days of limb suspension and 1 month of detraining in young non-runners [deboer-2007-ulls-tendon-time-course] [kubo-2012-achilles-training-detraining], and the bone timing statements are a review's summaries of other sources [warden-2021-bsi-optimal-workload].
- No retraining rate for recreational or masters runners returning to a prior peak, and no running trial of any progression rule other than the novice 10% programme.
