# Strength and plyometric training for runners

## Summary for the plan engine

Sixteen entries survived verification (13 of the researcher's 14 plus three that were added: [berryman-2018-strength-performance-meta], [denadai-2017-explosive-heavy-economy], [storen-2008-maximal-strength-economy]). Nothing was dropped: every DOI resolved, every title, journal and first author matched Crossref, and every number was found in the Europe PMC abstract. The corrections were wording, not substance. [giovanelli-2017-ultra-sep] is not described as randomized because the abstract only says the runners were divided into groups; the bone-stress figure in [toresdahl-2020-nyc-marathon-rct] was corrected (20 of the 52 overuse major injuries); and several descriptors that were not in the abstracts were removed.

Added 2026-10-25: twenty papers from one gap pass on the strength design (progression, effort targets, maintenance and stopping, women, masters athletes, calf raises, jumps, estimating a maximum, injury rates in the lifting sports). Their section is below; each was looked up on Crossref and its abstract read on Europe PMC the same day.

## Well established

- **Strength work improves running economy in trained distance runners, modestly.** The meta-analyses agree on direction. [denadai-2017-explosive-heavy-economy] gives about -3.9% oxygen cost; [llanoslagos-2024-economy-speeds] gives small to moderate effects (ES -0.27 to -0.43); [eihara-2022-heavy-vs-plyo] gives g of about -0.3. [balsalobre-2016-running-economy-highly-trained] reports SMD -1.42, but it rests on five trials and is an upper bound, not a planning figure.
- **Time-trial benefit, but not through VO2max or threshold.** [llanoslagos-2024-performance-methods], [blagrove-2018-strength-determinants] and [berryman-2018-strength-performance-meta] credit strength with performance gains via economy, force and power. No method moved VO2max, vVO2max or maximum metabolic steady state.
- **Heavy (high-load) lifting is the best-supported primary method**, with plyometrics as the complement. Submaximal circuits and isometrics did not improve economy in [llanoslagos-2024-economy-speeds]. [berryman-2018-strength-performance-meta] found maximal-force training better than other intensities.
- **Stretching does not prevent injuries** [lauersen-2014-exercise-injury-prevention].

## Contested

- **Heavy versus plyometric.** [eihara-2022-heavy-vs-plyo] favors heavy loads; [llanoslagos-2024-economy-speeds] shows plyometrics help at 12 km/h or slower; the combined-methods effect is the largest but low certainty. The engine should combine both rather than pick one.
- **Dose.** Reviews recommend 2-3 sessions per week for 8-12 weeks [blagrove-2018-strength-determinants], [balsalobre-2016-running-economy-highly-trained]. But [llanoslagos-2024-performance-methods] found no moderating effect of weeks, sessions per week or total sessions, and the duration subgroup in [eihara-2022-heavy-vs-plyo] has overlapping intervals. Longer is directionally better [denadai-2017-explosive-heavy-economy]; the exact threshold is not established.
- **Injury prevention.** Sports-general meta-analyses show RR about 0.32-0.34 for strength training [lauersen-2014-exercise-injury-prevention], [lauersen-2018-strength-dose-dependent], but they are not running-specific and have few trials. The one large running RCT was null [toresdahl-2020-nyc-marathon-rct], though its program was short, self-directed and low-load.
- **Interference.** [wilson-2012-concurrent-interference] found running specifically reduced strength and power gains as endurance volume grew; [schumann-2022-concurrent-compatibility] found no loss of maximal strength or hypertrophy and no age or frequency moderation, only attenuated explosive strength when sessions are in the same session. Neither measured running performance.

## Unknown

No trial studied 100-mile or mountain/trail runners, downhill or eccentric strength, or maintenance doses in runners. Women appear in one mixed 17-person trial [storen-2008-maximal-strength-economy], a mostly female novice injury trial and, since 2026-10-25, two small women-only trials with mixed results [vikmoen-2016-female-heavy-strength-running, vikmoen-2017-female-duathletes-strength]. Masters evidence is two small trials [piacentini-2013-masters-runners], [eihara-2024-middle-aged-pt-rt]; neither includes runners above about 50. There is no evidence on how strength interacts with taper timing, or on adverse effects at very high weekly mileage.

## Added 2026-10-25: progression, effort, maintenance and the other gaps

Twenty papers were added for the twelve gaps of the strength design (`docs/superpowers/specs/2026-10-24-strength-phase3b-design.md`, section 4.4), from a search of Crossref and Europe PMC by gap. Each was looked up on Crossref and its abstract read on Europe PMC (one candidate with no abstract in either source was left out); `npm run research:verify` found no mismatch. They add something on ten of the twelve gaps, close none of them, and found nothing for the eccentric dose in trail runners (gap 7) or for 100-mile runners (gap 10). They give no dose above grade D.

**Progression and effort (gaps 1 and 2).** A consensus stand for healthy adults recommends a 2-10% increase in load when the lifter can do the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number [acsm-2009-progression-models-resistance]. In 15 studies of resistance-trained lifters, effort-based (repetitions in reserve or velocity) and percentage-based load prescription gave similar 1RM gains [hickmott-2022-autoregulation-meta], and across trials strength gain did not depend on the estimated repetitions in reserve while muscle growth was larger nearer the limit of a set [robinson-2024-proximity-failure-meta]. The count is imprecise: lifters underpredicted the repetitions left by 0.95 on average (12 studies, 414 participants), less so closer to the limit [halperin-2021-rir-prediction-accuracy], and the effort rating relates to bar speed in squatters [zourdos-2016-rir-rpe-scale]. None of these studies runners and none tests double progression.

**Estimating a maximum (gap 11).** The repetitions possible at a percentage of the maximum fall along a curve and vary between people and exercises (269 studies, 7289 people), with little influence of sex, age or training status [nuzzo-2023-reps-at-percent-1rm]. In 70 adults the load fell nonlinearly with repetitions to failure, and the authors advised no more than 10 repetitions in a linear equation [reynolds-2006-1rm-prediction]. No paper validates the engine's equation; it stays an estimate.

**Keeping and losing strength (gaps 3 and 8).** In untrained adults a third or a ninth of the training dose kept muscle gained in the young, and strength was largely retained through 32 weeks of detraining [bickel-2011-maintenance-dosing-retention]. In resistance-trained men one set, one to three times a week, still raised squat 1RM [androulakis-2019-minimum-effective-dose]. For stopping, 8 middle-distance runners kept an economy gain over 4 weeks without explosive strength work [berryman-2020-strength-cessation-runners], while 14 elite cyclists fell behind a control group over 8 weeks without strength work [ronnestad-2016-strength-cessation-cyclists]. No trial tests lifting in a taper.

**Women and masters athletes (gaps 4 and 9).** In 19 well-trained women, 11 weeks of heavy lower-body training raised 1RM by 40% and changed neither running economy nor a 40-minute run [vikmoen-2016-female-heavy-strength-running]; in 19 female duathletes a 5-minute run after 1.5 hours of running improved by 4.7% against no change [vikmoen-2017-female-duathletes-strength]. Nine master endurance athletes (mean age 51.5) raised knee-extensor torque 17.8% in 3 weeks, with the efficiency outcome measured on a bicycle [louis-2011-master-athletes-strength-cycling]. No running trial covers a runner over about 50.

**Calf raises, jumps and injury rates (gaps 5, 6 and 12).** Standing calf raises grew the gastrocnemius more than seated raises and the soleus about as much, in 14 untrained adults, with muscle volume as the outcome [kinoshita-2023-standing-seated-calf-raise]. An exhausting weighted calf-raise protocol did not change modelled tibial stress in the run that followed, in 14 recreational runners [rice-2020-calf-raise-tibial-stress]. A pooled analysis of 21 studies shows that jump training improves economy and 2-5 km time trials in endurance runners, with no dose given [ramirezcampillo-2021-jump-training-runners]; landing forces of depth drops and jumps from 60 to 90 cm were higher than a vertical jump's in 14 elite track and field men [wallace-2010-plyometric-grf]. Injury rates in competitive weightlifting and powerlifting are in the range of other non-contact sports [aasa-2016-weightlifter-powerlifter-injuries, keogh-2016-weight-training-injuries]; these are competitive lifters and most studies are of low quality, so they are not safety data for a recreational runner starting barbell lifts.

**What they support.** The direction of a double-progression rule, the use of an effort count with its error stated, a low maintenance dose and a cautious return, the plyometric half of the block in runners, a modest, honestly bounded benefit sentence, and the statement that evidence in women is small and mixed.

**What they do not support.** A progression scheme, an effort target, a maintenance, taper, jump or calf dose, or an estimate of a maximum validated in runners; any claim for runners over about 50, for 100-mile runners or for eccentric work in trail runners; any claim that barbell lifting is safe for recreational runners. The calf-raise trial does not back alternating straight-knee and bent-knee raises. These gaps stand and the engine's values for them stay grade D defaults.

## How the engine should use this

1. Schedule 2 sessions per week by default, 10-14 weeks per block, heavy plus plyometric, then carry 1 maintenance session per week through the rest of the year, including peak mileage.
2. Never project a VO2max or threshold change from strength. Present expected economy gain as about 2-4% after a full block, with no race-time promise.
3. Place plyometric and heavy sessions on easy or intensity days, 3 hours or more away from key runs, and never directly before long runs.
4. Include strength in every plan for a mix of reasons, but make the injury claim modest and keep load progression as the primary injury control, especially for novices.
5. Use the same strength prescription for masters runners, with a longer ramp-in; for ultra and trail runners, emphasize slow-speed plyometrics and durability.
6. Every number that is a design default rather than a trial finding (sets, reps, ramp-in, maintenance dose) is graded D in the rules and should be user-configurable.
7. The load of each lift follows the runner's own logged sets by written rules (F8): double progression inside the repetition range, a step the equipment can make, a lower load after a set below the range, and no increase on a missing count of repetitions in reserve. State that no study compares ways of choosing the next load.
8. The estimated maximum is an estimate, never a test, and is used only for a first load, a ceiling and a labelled figure.
9. A maintenance, lighter-week or taper session is a default and not a requirement; the evidence on stopping is small and mixed.
10. The benefit sentence says "may", gives the oxygen-cost range and says nothing about race time; it states that the evidence in women and in 100-mile runners is small or thin.
11. Make no claim that barbell lifting is safe for recreational runners.

## Gaps

- No trial or review of 100-mile, mountain or trail runners; ultra evidence is one 25-person study of male ultra-marathoners.
- No evidence on downhill, eccentric or trail-specific strength work.
- Maintenance and stopping evidence comes from non-athletes, trained men and elite cyclists, plus one case-type study of 8 runners [berryman-2020-strength-cessation-runners]; the 1 session per week maintenance rule is a design choice.
- Two small women-only trials with mixed results [vikmoen-2016-female-heavy-strength-running, vikmoen-2017-female-duathletes-strength] and almost no female masters data; male-only samples in [giovanelli-2017-ultra-sep] and [zanini-2025-re-durability].
- Masters evidence is two small trials of runners about 43-45 years; nothing for runners above about 50.
- No running-specific injury RCT with a properly dosed, supervised heavy program; injury evidence is sports-general and the single running RCT used a short low-load self-directed program.
- Concurrent-training interference has not been measured against running performance or taper timing.
- No optimal set and rep scheme or frequency comparison within runners; the dose defaults are expert-style choices.
- No comparison of ways of progressing the load, and no test of a repetitions-in-reserve target, in runners; the nearest evidence is in resistance-trained lifters [hickmott-2022-autoregulation-meta, robinson-2024-proximity-failure-meta].
- No calf-raise dose (sets, repetitions, load, tempo, knee angle) and no ground-contact dose, progression or bone-stress data for runners.
- No validation of a repetitions-to-maximum equation in runners [nuzzo-2023-reps-at-percent-1rm, reynolds-2006-1rm-prediction], and no safety data for recreational runners starting barbell lifts [aasa-2016-weightlifter-powerlifter-injuries, keogh-2016-weight-training-injuries].
